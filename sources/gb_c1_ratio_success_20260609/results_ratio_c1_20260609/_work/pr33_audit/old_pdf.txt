Abstract
Graph-free node classification enables prediction from node attributes when graph rela-
tions are unavailable or costly to access at deployment. However, existing noise-robust
graph methods generally retain graph-dependent inference, whereas graph-free methods
have largely been studied with clean training labels. To address this gap, we propose
Granular Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision (GB-LCSS) for transductive node
classification with noisy labels and graph-free inference. GB-LCSS learns node repre-
sentations through label-free graph contrastive learning, freezes them, and recursively
groups nodes into granular balls. For each training node, the observed labels of the
other training nodes in its ball form a leave-one-out consensus. A weight determined by
their agreement blends this consensus with the node’s observed label to produce a soft
target for a multilayer perceptron (MLP). This design decouples region construction
from noisy-label aggregation and uses local evidence to reduce the influence of isolated
annotation errors. Across five datasets and five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves the
highest five-seed mean accuracy in 24 of the 25 settings, compared with seven noisy-
label baselines that use the same deployed MLP architecture. Its soft targets also have
lower mean Brier scores than the observed one-hot labels in all 25 settings.
Keywords: label noise, node classification, graph-free inference, graph contrastive
learning, granular ball computing, soft labels

1. Introduction
Node classification is a fundamental task in graph learning, in which node attributes
and relationships jointly support prediction. Graph neural networks (GNNs) combine
these two sources of information by aggregating representations from neighboring
nodes [1]. This dependence on graph structure, however, complicates deployment: each
prediction requires access to the relevant neighborhood, and retrieving and aggregating
multi-hop neighbor information adds computational cost. In applications with missing
relations, privacy restrictions, or low-latency requirements, such access may be unavail-
able or impractical. These constraints have motivated graph-free node classification,
in which graph structure is used during training but the deployed classifier receives
only node attributes. Graph-MLP [2] and GLNN [3] demonstrated this approach, and
later work such as SimMLP [4] and FF-G2M [5] refined what structural knowledge
is transferred to the multilayer perceptron (MLP) and how. These methods have been
developed and evaluated with clean training labels.
In practice, however, training labels obtained through manual annotation, automated
rules, or multiple sources may contain errors, and deep models can fit such corrupted
annotations [6]. On graphs, message passing may propagate the influence of mislabeled
nodes to their neighbors [7]. Noise-robust graph methods use topology to augment
supervision [8], regulate training with noisy labels [9], or select reliable samples [10],
but they retain graph access at inference. Graph-free classifiers face a related challenge:
errors in the labels used to train an MLP or its teacher can carry over to the deployed
model. We study how the training graph can improve noisy-label supervision for a
classifier that uses only node attributes at inference.
One way to address this challenge is to combine an observed label with evidence
from nearby training nodes. When their labels provide consistent evidence for the un-
derlying class, aggregating them can reduce the influence of isolated annotation errors.
Related methods use neighborhood consistency to identify potentially mislabeled sam-
ples or encourage agreement between neighboring predictions [11, 12]. For graph data,
a graph contrastive representation learned from node attributes and connectivity without
class labels provides a basis for identifying such local groups [13]. The remaining
2

PAGE 3
challenge is to form local regions from the representation geometry before aggregating
labels, so that annotation errors do not determine which nodes contribute evidence.
Granular ball computing offers one way to construct such regions. It groups samples
into coarse-grained units summarized by their centers and radii [14]. Subsequent studies
have investigated granular ball sampling for noisy or imbalanced classification [15] and
refined adaptive ball generation [16]. Unlike supervised granular ball classifiers that
use label purity to guide splitting, GB-LCSS forms balls from representation geometry
under size and ball-count constraints before aggregating training labels.
Building on this idea, we propose Granular Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision
(GB-LCSS) for transductive node classification with noisy labels and graph-free infer-
ence. As shown in Fig. 1, GB-LCSS first learns node representations through label-free
graph contrastive learning and freezes them. It then recursively applies 2-means to
the frozen node representations, assigning each node to exactly one granular ball. For
each training node, the observed labels of the other training nodes in its ball provide a
leave-one-out consensus. A weight determined by their agreement blends this consensus
with the node’s observed label to form a soft target. An MLP trained on these targets
receives only node attributes at inference.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
(1) We introduce GB-LCSS for noisy-label node classification with graph-free in-
ference. It organizes nodes into granular balls in a frozen, label-free graph
representation, thereby separating region construction from noisy-label aggrega-
tion.
(2) We construct node-specific soft targets by combining each observed label with the
consensus of the other training nodes in its ball. Under independent symmetric
label noise, we analyze how the number of votes and the underlying class distribu-
tion affect the reliability of local voting. We also derive an exact decomposition
of the squared error of the soft target and a sufficient condition under which it has
lower expected error than the observed label.
(3) We evaluate GB-LCSS on five datasets under five noise settings against noisy-
label learning methods and graph-assisted MLP methods. Controlled comparisons

2. Related work
2.1. Learning with noisy labels
Methods for learning with noisy labels reduce the influence of corrupted annotations
by selecting training samples, encouraging consistent predictions, or modifying the
training objective. Sample-selection methods use the training loss to assess label
reliability: Co-teaching [17] exchanged small-loss samples between two networks, and
4

PAGE 5
JoCoR [18] additionally encouraged prediction agreement. Local relationships offer
another source of evidence. Deep k-NN [12] filtered out samples whose labels disagreed
with those of their neighbors, while neighbor-consistency regularization [11] encouraged
the prediction for each sample to match those of its neighbors.
Other methods address label noise by modifying the training loss. SCE [19] com-
bined cross-entropy with reverse cross-entropy, ELR [6] regularized training to limit
noise memorization, and LogitClip [20] constrained logit norms. These strategies handle
annotation noise without explicitly modeling graph relations between samples.
2.2. Noise-robust learning on graphs
Graph structure can provide additional evidence for assessing label reliability, while
message passing may also spread the influence of supervision errors [7]. UnionNET
[21] used neighborhood-based label estimates for reweighting and correction, NRGNN
[8] augmented supervision through structural information and pseudo-labeling, and
RTGNN [9] coordinated two networks to regulate noisy supervision.
Label reliability can also be assessed through graph topology and model behavior.
TSS [10] used topological sample selection, ProCon [22] used peer-informed label
consistency, and GNN-CFGD [23] combined coarse- and fine-grained sample division
to address label noise and sparsity.
Beyond assessing label reliability, several methods have constructed additional su-
pervision. DND-NET [24] used neighborhood uncertainty to reweight pseudo-label su-
pervision, and RNCGLN [25] combined graph contrastive learning with pseudo-graphs
and pseudo-labels to address graph and label noise. Prototype-Guided Supervision
[26] formed class-level prototypes by aggregating the representations of nodes with the
same observed label, while SAMTD [27] improved supervision through multiple graph
teachers.
Other methods modify neighborhood aggregation or the training objective. MILAB
[28] combined information-bottleneck-guided aggregation with adversarial consistency,
and HALF [29] fused low- and high-pass losses according to local homophily to identify
reliable labels. In addition, NoisyGL [7] established a unified evaluation framework for
learning with noisy labels on graphs.

2.3. Graph-free node classification
Methods for graph-free node classification exploit graph information during training
while avoiding online message passing at inference. Graph-MLP [2] incorporated
adjacency information through a neighborhood-contrastive objective, whereas GLNN
[3] distilled predictions from a GNN teacher into an MLP. Both methods deploy attribute-
only MLPs. GSDN [30] used neighborhood self-distillation, SimMLP [4] aligned GNN
and MLP representations without supervision, and SA-MLP [31] investigated structure-
aware distillation. These methods differ in how graph information guides training and
in whether precomputed structural features are retained at inference.
Subsequent distillation methods differ in the teacher information they transfer. FF-
G2M [5] separated low- and high-frequency knowledge, KRD [32] assessed knowledge
reliability through the stability of prediction entropy under noise perturbations, and
VQGraph [33] encoded local structures as discrete tokens. PGKD [34] used class
prototypes to guide structural knowledge distillation from GNNs to MLPs, whereas
TINED [35] combined teacher parameter injection with Dirichlet-energy distillation.
SALE-MLP [36] investigated structure-aware latent representations. Yu et al. [37]
theoretically analyzed how label alignment in distillation allows an MLP to learn the
implicit graph bias of a GNN teacher.
A related line of work uses graph structure to construct soft training targets. Pos-
teL [38] constructed posterior soft labels from neighborhood labels and graph-wide
label statistics. ALS [39] used neighborhood label distributions and adaptively up-
dated smoothed targets during graph training, while SALS [40] incorporated structural
information into the target label distribution. These methods use adjacency-defined
neighborhoods to construct or refine training targets. GB-LCSS also modifies the train-
ing targets, but it defines regions by proximity in a frozen, label-free representation and
excludes each node’s own label from the consensus that adjusts it.
2.4. Graph contrastive representation learning
Self-supervised graph learning derives training signals from node attributes and
graph connectivity [41]. Graph contrastive learning generates different views of a graph
by perturbing its structure or attributes. A common node-level objective brings the
6

PAGE 7
representations of the same node across views closer together while distinguishing
them from those of other nodes. The resulting node representations provide a basis for
comparing nodes without using their observed class labels.
GRACE [13] implements this approach through random edge dropping and feature
masking, with a shared graph encoder processing the two views. Because its objective
uses no class labels, GB-LCSS can compute and freeze the GRACE representation
before any training label is used; the observed training labels enter only afterwards,
when soft targets are constructed within the resulting groups.
2.5. Granular ball computing
Granular ball computing represents samples using coarse-grained hyperspherical
regions, which GBkNN used to classify query samples [14]. Granular ball sampling
extended this representation to sample selection for noisy or imbalanced classification
[15], and subsequent work refined adaptive ball generation [16]. MGNR [42] used
granular ball representations to construct multi-granularity neighborhood relationships
for classification and clustering.
On graphs, supervised granular ball coarsening [43] and GBGC [44] used granular
regions to construct coarsened graphs, reducing the size of the graph on which a GNN
operates. The methods above use granular regions for classification, clustering, sample
selection, or graph coarsening. In supervised granular ball classifiers and sampling
methods, label purity can guide ball generation [14–16]. In contrast, GB-LCSS uses
granular balls to organize supervision: a geometric criterion forms them in a frozen,
label-free graph representation, and the observed training labels are then aggregated
within each ball. Granular ball membership determines which other training nodes
contribute to the soft target of each node. These targets transfer graph information into
the supervision of an MLP that receives only node attributes at inference.

GB-LCSS.

3.6. Computational costs
Relative to direct MLP training, GB-LCSS adds three training-time stages: graph
contrastive pretraining, granular ball partitioning, and soft-target construction. The
pretraining cost depends on the encoder and the training schedule; Section 4.4.2 reports
17

PAGE 18
the measured cost. Pretraining is shared across downstream runs that use the same
frozen representation.
The cost of partitioning is dominated by the 2-means calls. Running 2-means for
I iterations on a ball Bj, with a fixed number of initializations, costs O(I |Bj| h). The
candidate balls examined in one pass of Algorithm 1 are disjoint, so their combined size
is at most N. Each pass either accepts one split or terminates the algorithm. There are at
most Bmax −1 successful passes and one final unsuccessful pass, so the total cost of all
2-means calls is bounded by
O(I Bmax N h).
(22)
For fixed I and Bmax, this bound is linear in both the number of nodes and the
representation dimension. Sorting the candidates by dispersion in each pass adds
at most O(B2
max log Bmax) operations in total. The frozen representation G requires
O(Nh) space, and the node assignments, ball centers, and per-ball class counts require
O(N + Bmax(h +C)) space. Given the per-ball class counts, computing the targets for the
labeled nodes takes O(|VL|C) time. The implementation initializes and stores a dense
N × C target matrix, which adds O(NC) time and space.
The classifier is the same two-layer MLP as that used in the baselines. Let F denote
its hidden width and TS the number of training epochs. The loss in Eq. (13) involves
only the training nodes, but the full-batch implementation evaluates all N nodes in each
epoch, at a total cost of O(TS N(dF + FC)).
At inference, the cost per node is
O(dF + FC).

The model parameters also require O(dF + FC) space. The extension additionally
trains a GCN teacher and then trains an MLP student on the teacher’s predictions for all
N nodes. Its deployed MLP has the same architecture and inference cost as above.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental setup
4.1.1. Datasets
The main experiments use five public datasets: the citation graphs Cora and CiteSeer
[46], the 70-class citation graph CoraFull, the co-authorship graph Coauthor-CS, and
the co-purchase graph Amazon-Photo [47]. Table 2 summarizes their statistics. Edges
are counted as unique undirected pairs after self-loop removal, training-node counts
refer to the 20% split, and edge homophily is the proportion of edges that connect
nodes of the same class. Six additional datasets are evaluated under uniform 0.4 noise
(Supplementary Appendix G).
4.1.2. Data splits and label noise
All comparisons follow the fixed-graph transductive protocol of Section 3.1. For
each seed from 0 to 4, we use the NoisyGL percentage-split routine to randomly sample
training, validation, and test nodes from the full node set at ratios of 20%, 10%, and 70%,
respectively. Noise is injected only into the training labels, and all methods share the
same split and noisy labels for each dataset and seed. Clean validation labels are used
for checkpoint selection, and test labels for final evaluation. Supplementary Appendix F
gives the exact subset sizes and the sampling procedure, and Supplementary Appendix C
examines sensitivity to corrupted validation labels.
Under uniform noise, each training label is retained with probability 1 −η and is
replaced by each of the other C −1 classes with probability η/(C −1). We evaluate η ∈
{0.2, 0.4, 0.6}. Pair noise uses η = 0.4: it changes class i ∈{1, . . . ,C} to ((i−2) mod C)+1
with probability η and leaves it unchanged with probability 1 −η. Instance-dependent
19

PAGE 20
noise is generated with the NoisyGL generator, using a configured mean flip rate of 0.4
and norm_std = 0.1.
4.1.3. Compared methods
The first comparison group comprises seven noisy-label baselines: MLP+CE, Small-
loss, Co-teaching, JoCoR, SCE, ELR, and LogitClip. They use the same deployed
two-layer MLP and the same labeled training nodes as GB-LCSS. GB-LCSS uses graph
information during training and requires no noise-model parameters. Small-loss, Co-
teaching, and JoCoR use the true noise rate to set their forget-rate schedules. Small-loss
selects samples by their per-sample cross-entropy. Co-teaching and JoCoR train two
independently initialized MLPs but evaluate a single network without ensembling.
The second group comprises graph-assisted MLP methods: PosteL [38], ALS [39],
SALS [40], Graph-MLP [2], and GSDN [30]. All of them use graph information only
during training in our protocol. PosteL uses the official target-construction code with
iterative pseudo-labeling disabled. Graph-MLP is adapted to the common MLP, whereas
ALS, SALS, and GSDN are implemented as mechanism adaptations under the common
protocol. Supplementary Appendix F identifies the retained components and parameter
sources for each method.
The third group comprises graph-to-MLP distillation methods: a GCN(X)+CE
response-distillation baseline based on GLNN [3], FF-G2M [5], KRD [32], VQGraph
[33], and a GCN teacher trained on GB-LCSS targets (Section 3.5). FF-G2M is reim-
plemented with the common backbones and its low- and high-frequency objectives.
The mechanism adaptations of KRD and VQGraph retain reliability-based knowledge
selection and soft-codebook distillation, respectively. For each seed, the teacher and
student are retrained for every route, using the same MLP configuration for all students.
4.1.4. Implementation details
Datasets are loaded through the NoisyGL interface to PyTorch Geometric. Graph
contrastive pretraining uses row-normalized attributes, whereas downstream models
use the attributes supplied by NoisyGL. Graph-convolutional models use the supplied
20

PAGE 21
adjacency, with normalization and self-loops handled within their graph-convolution
layers.
The noisy-label baselines and GB-LCSS use a two-layer MLP with a hidden width
of 64 and a dropout rate of 0.5. We train for 200 epochs using Adam with a learning rate
of 0.01 and a weight decay of 5 × 10−4, and we select the checkpoint with the highest
validation accuracy. Teacher checkpoints are selected with the same criterion.
By default, GB-LCSS uses Bmax = 64, nmin = 32, δ = 0.05, smin = 2, and β = 0.5.
Recursive 2-means uses Euclidean distance, k-means++ initialization [48], and 10
initializations per candidate split, with randomization controlled by the downstream
seed. The distillation extension uses a temperature of τ = 1. GRACE configurations and
all other parameter settings, with their sources, are given in Supplementary Appendix F.
4.1.5. Evaluation protocol
Test-node classification accuracy is reported in percent as the mean ± sample
standard deviation over five paired seeds. Bold marks the highest mean within each
dataset and noise setting; underlining marks the second-highest mean in the graph-
assisted comparison. Each dataset uses one fixed normalized pretrained representation
G. Section 4.3.3 separately examines variation across GRACE pretraining seeds.
Same-seed accuracy differences are assessed with two-sided paired t-tests and 95%
confidence intervals. Holm correction [49] is applied separately to each family of
25 tests, with one family for each reference method: random size-matched grouping,
MLP+CE, and JoCoR. Supplementary Appendix B reports the paired comparisons,
confidence intervals, and Holm-adjusted p-values.

4.3.2. Sensitivity to the grouping rule
This experiment tests whether local-consensus supervision depends on the specific
partitioning procedure. We keep the frozen representation and Eqs. (7)–(12) unchanged
and replace the granular ball partition with kNN neighborhoods or a k-means partition.
For each seed, kNN excludes the query node and matches its neighborhood size to the
mean number of training votes per ball; k-means uses the same number of groups as
the granular ball partition and likewise excludes the current node’s own vote. Table 6
reports the comparison under uniform 0.4, with differences defined as the accuracy of
the alternative grouping minus that of granular ball grouping, in percentage points.
The three grouping rules yield mean accuracies within one percentage point of one
another on four of the five datasets (Table 6). All three exceed random size-matched
grouping (Tables 5 and 6). These results show that local-consensus targets can also
be constructed with other grouping rules. We use granular balls because geometric
splitting adapts the partition under explicit size and ball-count constraints, while each
ball provides shared class counts for constructing its members’ targets. Section 4.4.2
examines the construction time and storage requirements of this design.

points on Coauthor-CS and 0.99 points on Amazon-Photo.
On Cora, CiteSeer, and CoraFull, we train three GRACE representations with
different pretraining seeds and evaluate each representation using three downstream
seeds. Partitions obtained from different pretraining seeds differ substantially, with
adjusted Rand indices [51] between 0.247 and 0.365. After averaging over downstream
seeds, the standard deviations of accuracy across pretraining seeds are 0.13, 0.45,
and 0.60 percentage points, respectively, and GB-LCSS exceeds MLP+CE for every
pretraining seed. Thus, although the node assignments change substantially across
pretraining seeds, downstream accuracy changes little.
4.3.4. Sensitivity to construction and target parameters
Supplementary Appendix I.1 varies one grouping or voting parameter at a time on
the five datasets under uniform 0.4 noise, with fixed representations and five paired
seeds. The largest losses occur when a higher splitting threshold leaves all nodes in one
ball or when a higher voting threshold sharply reduces the number of nodes receiving
consensus supervision. The remaining tested settings stay within three percentage points

4.4. Graph-teacher extension and computational cost
4.4.1. Graph-teacher distillation
We next examine whether training a graph teacher with GB-LCSS targets also
improves its distilled MLP student (Section 3.5). Table 8 compares direct MLP train-
ing with five distillation routes on the five datasets under uniform 0.4 and 0.6. The
first row trains the MLP on the labeled nodes only, whereas the remaining rows use
teacher predictions with method-specific objectives and node selection; all routes deploy
the same two-layer MLP. The GCN teacher trained on GB-LCSS targets differs from
the GCN(X)+CE baseline only in using the soft target of Eq. (12) in place of the ob-
served one-hot label; the other routes are described in Section 4.1.3 and Supplementary
Appendix F.
Training the GCN teacher with GB-LCSS targets instead of the observed labels
raises the mean accuracy of the distilled student in nine of the ten settings; the only
exception is CoraFull under uniform 0.4. Target refinement can therefore improve the
supervision transferred through a graph teacher. Among the five distillation routes, this
student attains the highest mean accuracy in eight of the ten settings; on CoraFull and
Amazon-Photo under uniform 0.4, GCN(X)+CE and VQGraph are higher by 1.45 and
0.14 points, respectively. The distilled student exceeds direct MLP training in all ten
settings, by 3.12–23.86 points under uniform 0.4 and 5.21–29.48 points under uniform
0.6. The extension adds a GCN teacher and distillation over all nodes during training,
while retaining the same MLP architecture at inference.

4.5. Discussion
When the other training labels in a ball provide consistent evidence for the underlying
class, their consensus can offset an isolated annotation error. The label-free represen-
tation determines which nodes contribute this evidence, and the random-grouping and
label-smoothing controls support the importance of this choice beyond target softening
alone (Table 5). Leave-one-out voting keeps the current annotation out of its own con-
sensus estimate, a property used in the conditional expected-error analysis of Corollary 1.
Under uniform 0.4 noise, the reduction in Brier error on corrupted labels exceeds the
error introduced on correctly labeled nodes (Supplementary Appendix I.2).
The usefulness of local evidence depends on both its class composition and the
number of available votes. Proposition 1 relates these quantities to voting reliability
under independent symmetric noise. Agreement alone is insufficient when the local
labels favor an incorrect class, since the consensus can then move the target away from
the true class. The representation and parameter studies show lower accuracy when
local groups fail to form or voting support becomes sparse. Under uniform 0.4 noise,
CoraFull has both the lowest proportion of training nodes with a positive clean-vote
margin (36.13%) and the smallest net reduction in target Brier score among the five
datasets (Supplementary Appendix I.2). With clean training labels, refinement raises
30

PAGE 31
mean accuracy on one dataset and lowers it on four, with all changes within 2.00
percentage points (Supplementary Appendix D). On the six additional datasets, GB-
LCSS exceeds the strongest noisy-label baseline on three (Supplementary Appendix G).
These results indicate that the benefit varies across datasets and supervision conditions.
GB-LCSS, Graph-MLP, and GLNN share an attribute-only deployment interface
but use the training graph differently. Graph-MLP shapes the representation through a
neighborhood-contrastive objective, GLNN transfers predictions from a GNN teacher,
and GB-LCSS refines supervision targets. GB-LCSS and Graph-MLP have dataset-
dependent advantages (Table 4). The distillation experiment shows that training the same
GCN teacher with GB-LCSS targets can also benefit its MLP student (Table 8). These
targets can therefore support both direct MLP training and graph-to-MLP distillation.
Granular balls provide a practical organization for local-consensus supervision.
Each node belongs to one ball, whose class counts are shared when constructing targets
for its training nodes. Geometric splitting adapts the partition to the representation while
limiting the number of balls and enforcing a minimum size for each child ball. Granular
ball grouping requires less storage than exact kNN on the three larger graphs and has
the shortest construction time on three datasets (Fig. 5). These results support its use as
a grouping mechanism that combines competitive accuracy, shared regional statistics,
and controlled construction costs.
5. Conclusion
We presented GB-LCSS for learning from noisy training labels when the deployed
classifier receives only node attributes. The method organizes nodes into granular balls
in a frozen, label-free graph representation and combines each observed label with
the consensus of the other training nodes in its ball. Our analysis relates local-vote
reliability to the number of available votes and their clean-class margin under symmetric
noise, decomposes the squared error of the soft target, and gives a sufficient condition
for it to have lower expected error than the observed label.
Across five datasets and five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves the highest five-seed
mean accuracy in 24 of the 25 settings, compared with seven noisy-label baselines that
31

PAGE 32
use the same deployed MLP architecture. Its soft targets have lower mean Brier scores
than the observed one-hot labels in all 25 settings. The controlled comparisons support
the importance of which training nodes contribute to the local consensus, with granular
balls providing a compact way to share label statistics. The distillation experiments
further show that these targets can improve the MLP student when they are used to train
its graph teacher.
The present evaluation focuses on synthetic label noise in a fixed-graph transductive
setting, with clean validation labels used for model selection. Future work will test the
method on naturally occurring annotation errors and develop model-selection procedures
for imperfect validation labels. Another direction is to evaluate generalization to newly
arriving nodes and to investigate how the representations and granular ball partitions
can be updated as the training graph grows.
