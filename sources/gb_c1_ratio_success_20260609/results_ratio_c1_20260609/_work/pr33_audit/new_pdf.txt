Abstract
Graph-free node classification predicts from node attributes when graph relations are
unavailable or costly at deployment. Existing noise-robust graph methods retain graph-
dependent inference, whereas graph-free methods largely assume clean labels. We
propose Granular Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision (GB-LCSS) for transductive
node classification with noisy labels and graph-free inference. GB-LCSS learns label-
free graph contrastive representations, freezes them, and recursively groups nodes into
granular balls. For each training node, labels of the other training nodes in its ball form
a leave-one-out consensus whose agreement determines how strongly it is blended with
the observed label to form a soft target for a multilayer perceptron (MLP). This separates
region construction from noisy-label aggregation and reduces the influence of isolated
annotation errors. Across five datasets and five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves the
highest five-seed mean accuracy in 24 of 25 settings among seven noisy-label baselines
sharing the same deployed MLP. Its soft targets also have lower mean Brier scores than
the observed one-hot labels in all 25 settings.
Keywords: label noise, node classification, graph-free inference, graph contrastive
learning, granular ball computing, soft labels

1. Introduction
Node classification uses node attributes and graph relations for prediction. Graph
neural networks (GNNs) combine them by aggregating neighboring representations [1],
but this graph dependence complicates deployment because each prediction requires
neighborhood access and message passing. Such access may be unavailable under
missing relations, privacy restrictions, or low-latency requirements. Graph-free node
classification instead uses graph structure during training while deploying an attribute-
only classifier. Graph-MLP [2] and GLNN [3] established this setting, and later methods
such as SimMLP [4] and FF-G2M [5] refined the transferred structural knowledge.
These methods have largely been evaluated with clean training labels.
In practice, however, training labels obtained through manual annotation, automated
rules, or multiple sources may contain errors, and deep models can fit such corrupted
annotations [6]. On graphs, message passing may propagate the influence of mislabeled
nodes to their neighbors [7]. Noise-robust graph methods use topology to augment
supervision [8], regulate training with noisy labels [9], or select reliable samples [10],
but they retain graph access at inference. Graph-free classifiers face a related challenge:
errors in the labels used to train an MLP or its teacher can carry over to the deployed
model. We study how the training graph can improve noisy-label supervision for a
classifier that uses only node attributes at inference.
A natural strategy is to combine an observed label with evidence from nearby
training nodes, so consistent local labels can offset isolated errors. Related methods
use neighborhood consistency to identify mislabeled samples or encourage agreement
among nearby predictions [11, 12]. For graphs, label-free contrastive representations
provide a basis for identifying such local groups [13]. The key challenge is to form
these regions before aggregating labels, preventing annotation errors from determining
which nodes contribute evidence.
Granular ball computing provides such regions by grouping samples into coarse-
grained units summarized by centers and radii [14]. Later work studied granular ball
sampling for noisy or imbalanced classification [15] and adaptive ball generation [16].

GB-LCSS instead forms balls from representation geometry under size and ball-count
constraints before aggregating labels.
Building on this idea, we propose Granular Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision
(GB-LCSS) for transductive node classification with noisy labels and graph-free infer-
ence. As shown in Fig. 1, GB-LCSS first learns node representations through label-free
graph contrastive learning and freezes them. It then recursively applies 2-means to
the frozen node representations, assigning each node to exactly one granular ball. For
each training node, the observed labels of the other training nodes in its ball provide a
leave-one-out consensus. A weight determined by their agreement blends this consensus
with the node’s observed label to form a soft target. An MLP trained on these targets
receives only node attributes at inference.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
(1) We introduce GB-LCSS for noisy-label node classification with graph-free in-
ference. It organizes nodes into granular balls in a frozen, label-free graph

representation, thereby separating region construction from noisy-label aggrega-

(2) We construct node-specific soft targets by combining each observed label with the
consensus of the other training nodes in its ball. Under independent symmetric
label noise, we analyze how the number of votes and the underlying class distribu-
tion affect the reliability of local voting. We also derive an exact decomposition
of the squared error of the soft target and a sufficient condition under which it has
lower expected error than the observed label.
(3) We evaluate GB-LCSS on five datasets under five noise settings against noisy-
label learning methods and graph-assisted MLP methods. Controlled comparisons
examine grouping, representation learning, target construction, and parameter
sensitivity. A graph-teacher extension evaluates the same targets against graph-to-
MLP distillation methods.
2. Related work
2.1. Learning with noisy labels
Learning with noisy labels commonly relies on sample selection, consistency infor-
mation, or robust objectives. Co-teaching [17] exchanges small-loss samples between
two networks, while JoCoR [18] also enforces prediction agreement. Local relationships
provide complementary evidence: neighbor-consistency regularization [11] encourages
agreement among nearby samples, and the recent affinity-aware uncertainty framework
in Pattern Recognition [19] uses sample–prototype affinities to quantify uncertainty and
refine pseudo-labels. Loss-based approaches include SCE [20], ELR [6], and Logit-
Clip [21], which respectively symmetrize cross-entropy, limit noise memorization, and
constrain logit norms.
2.2. Noise-robust learning on graphs
Graph structure provides additional evidence for noisy-label learning, although
message passing can also propagate supervision errors [7]. NRGNN [8] augments super-
vision with structural information and pseudo-labeling, TSS [10] selects reliable nodes
4

PAGE 5
using topology, and RNCGLN [22] jointly addresses graph and label corruption. Recent
Pattern Recognition studies further exploit reliability-aware graph information: MILAB
[23] combines information-bottleneck-guided aggregation with adversarial consistency,
while confidence-calibrated node selection [24] propagates annotation reliability using
prototypes and graph structure. NoisyGL [7] provides a unified benchmark for graph
learning under label noise. These methods retain graph-dependent predictors, whereas
GB-LCSS uses the training graph to construct supervision for an attribute-only classifier
deployed without graph access.
2.3. Graph-free node classification
Graph-free node classification uses graph information during training while avoiding
online message passing at inference. Graph-MLP [2] adds a neighborhood-contrastive
objective, GLNN [3] distills a GNN teacher into an MLP, GSDN [25] uses neighbor-
hood self-distillation, and SimMLP [4] aligns GNN and MLP representations without
supervision. More recent distillation methods transfer different structural signals: FF-
G2M [5] separates low- and high-frequency knowledge, KRD [26] estimates knowledge
reliability under perturbations, and VQGraph [27] encodes local structures as discrete
tokens.
Graph structure can also define soft targets. PosteL [28] uses neighborhood labels
and graph-wide statistics, ALS [29] adaptively updates smoothed neighborhood targets,
and SALS [30] incorporates structural information into the target distribution. GB-LCSS
instead defines local regions in a frozen, label-free representation and excludes each
node’s own label from the consensus used to adjust it.
2.4. Graph contrastive representation learning
Self-supervised graph learning derives training signals from node attributes and
graph connectivity [31]. Graph contrastive learning generates different views of a graph
by perturbing its structure or attributes. A common node-level objective brings the
representations of the same node across views closer together while distinguishing
them from those of other nodes. The resulting node representations provide a basis for
comparing nodes without using their observed class labels.
5

PAGE 6
GRACE [13] implements this approach through random edge dropping and feature
masking, with a shared graph encoder processing the two views. Because its objective
uses no class labels, GB-LCSS can compute and freeze the GRACE representation
before any training label is used; the observed training labels enter only afterwards,
when soft targets are constructed within the resulting groups.
2.5. Granular ball computing
Granular ball computing represents samples with coarse-grained hyperspherical
regions. GBkNN uses such regions for classification [14], granular ball sampling
extends them to noisy or imbalanced data [15], and adaptive generation improves
their construction [16]. MGNR [32] further builds multi-granularity neighborhood
relationships from granular balls. More recently, BNO-GB in Pattern Recognition [33]
uses label-agnostic granular-ball clustering and multi-level consistency to identify noisy
samples in open intent classification.
On graphs, supervised granular ball coarsening [34] and GBGC [35] construct
coarsened graphs on which a GNN operates. These methods use granular regions for
classification, sample selection, or graph coarsening, and supervised variants may use
label purity to guide ball generation [14–16]. GB-LCSS instead forms balls geomet-
rically in a frozen, label-free graph representation and only then aggregates observed
training labels. Ball membership determines which nodes contribute to each soft target,
transferring graph information into supervision for an attribute-only MLP.

3.6. Computational costs
Relative to direct MLP training, GB-LCSS adds three training-time stages: graph
contrastive pretraining, granular ball partitioning, and soft-target construction. The
pretraining cost depends on the encoder and the training schedule; Section 4.4.2 reports
the measured cost. Pretraining is shared across downstream runs that use the same
frozen representation.
The cost of partitioning is dominated by the 2-means calls. Running 2-means for
I iterations on a ball Bj, with a fixed number of initializations, costs O(I |Bj| h). The
candidate balls examined in one pass of Algorithm 1 are disjoint, so their combined size
15

PAGE 16
is at most N. Each pass either accepts one split or terminates the algorithm. There are at
most Bmax −1 successful passes and one final unsuccessful pass, so the total cost of all
2-means calls is bounded by
O(I Bmax N h).
(22)
For fixed I and Bmax, this bound is linear in both the number of nodes and the
representation dimension. Sorting the candidates by dispersion in each pass adds
at most O(B2
max log Bmax) operations in total. The frozen representation G requires
O(Nh) space, and the node assignments, ball centers, and per-ball class counts require
O(N + Bmax(h +C)) space. Given the per-ball class counts, computing the targets for the
labeled nodes takes O(|VL|C) time. The implementation initializes and stores a dense
N × C target matrix, which adds O(NC) time and space.
Let F be the MLP hidden width and TS the number of epochs. The full-batch
implementation evaluates all N nodes per epoch, for total cost O(TS N(dF + FC)).
At inference, the cost per node is
O(dF + FC).
(23)
Model parameters require O(dF + FC) space. The extension additionally trains a
GCN teacher and an MLP student on all-node teacher predictions, while retaining the
same deployed MLP and inference cost.

4.1.2. Data splits and label noise
All comparisons follow the fixed-graph transductive protocol of Section 3.1. For
each seed from 0 to 4, we use the NoisyGL percentage-split routine to randomly sample
training, validation, and test nodes from the full node set at ratios of 20%, 10%, and 70%,
respectively. Noise is injected only into the training labels, and all methods share the
same split and noisy labels for each dataset and seed. Clean validation labels are used
for checkpoint selection, and test labels for final evaluation. Supplementary Appendix F
gives the exact subset sizes and the sampling procedure, and Supplementary Appendix C
examines sensitivity to corrupted validation labels.
Under uniform noise, each training label is retained with probability 1 −η and is
replaced by each of the other C −1 classes with probability η/(C −1). We evaluate η ∈
{0.2, 0.4, 0.6}. Pair noise uses η = 0.4: it changes class i ∈{1, . . . ,C} to ((i−2) mod C)+1
with probability η and leaves it unchanged with probability 1 −η. Instance-dependent
noise is generated with the NoisyGL generator, using a configured mean flip rate of 0.4
and norm_std = 0.1.
4.1.3. Compared methods
We compare with seven noisy-label baselines: MLP+CE, Small-loss, Co-teaching,
JoCoR, SCE, ELR, and LogitClip. All use the same deployed two-layer MLP and
labeled training nodes as GB-LCSS. Small-loss, Co-teaching, and JoCoR use the true
noise rate for their forget-rate schedules; the latter two train independent MLP pairs
but evaluate one network without ensembling. GB-LCSS requires no noise-model
parameters.
Graph-assisted MLP comparisons include PosteL [28], ALS [29], SALS [30], Graph-
MLP [2], and GSDN [25], all using graph information only during training. PosteL uses
17

the official target-construction code with iterative pseudo-labeling disabled; Graph-MLP
uses the common MLP, while ALS, SALS, and GSDN are mechanism adaptations.
Supplementary Appendix F details retained components and parameter sources.
Graph-to-MLP comparisons include a GCN(X)+CE response-distillation baseline
based on GLNN [3], FF-G2M [5], KRD [26], VQGraph [27], and a GCN teacher
trained on GB-LCSS targets (Section 3.5). FF-G2M retains its frequency-specific ob-
jectives, while KRD and VQGraph retain reliability-based selection and soft-codebook
distillation. Each route retrains teacher and student per seed with the same student MLP.
4.1.4. Implementation details
Datasets are loaded through the NoisyGL interface to PyTorch Geometric. Graph
contrastive pretraining uses row-normalized attributes, whereas downstream models
use the attributes supplied by NoisyGL. Graph-convolutional models use the supplied
adjacency, with normalization and self-loops handled within their graph-convolution
layers.
The noisy-label baselines and GB-LCSS use a two-layer MLP with a hidden width
of 64 and a dropout rate of 0.5. We train for 200 epochs using Adam with a learning rate
of 0.01 and a weight decay of 5 × 10−4, and we select the checkpoint with the highest
validation accuracy. Teacher checkpoints are selected with the same criterion.
By default, GB-LCSS uses Bmax = 64, nmin = 32, δ = 0.05, smin = 2, and β = 0.5.
Recursive 2-means uses Euclidean distance, k-means++ initialization [39], and 10
initializations per candidate split, with randomization controlled by the downstream
seed. The distillation extension uses a temperature of τ = 1. GRACE configurations and
all other parameter settings, with their sources, are given in Supplementary Appendix F.

seeds. Bold and underline mark the highest and second-highest means, respectively,
where applicable. Each dataset uses one fixed normalized pretrained representation G;
Section 4.3.3 examines variation across GRACE pretraining seeds.

4.3.2. Sensitivity to the grouping rule

grouping (Tables 5 and 6). These results show that local-consensus targets can also

The usefulness of local evidence depends on both its class composition and the
number of available votes. Proposition 1 relates these quantities to voting reliability
under independent symmetric noise. Agreement alone is insufficient when the local
labels favor an incorrect class, since the consensus can then move the target away from
the true class. The representation and parameter studies show lower accuracy when
local groups fail to form or voting support becomes sparse. Under uniform 0.4 noise,
CoraFull has both the lowest proportion of training nodes with a positive clean-vote
margin (36.13%) and the smallest net reduction in target Brier score among the five
datasets (Supplementary Appendix I.2). With clean training labels, refinement raises
mean accuracy on one dataset and lowers it on four, with all changes within 2.00
percentage points (Supplementary Appendix D). On the six additional datasets, GB-
LCSS exceeds the strongest noisy-label baseline on three (Supplementary Appendix G).
These results indicate that the benefit varies across datasets and supervision conditions.
GB-LCSS, Graph-MLP, and GLNN all deploy attribute-only models but use the
training graph differently: Graph-MLP shapes representations, GLNN transfers teacher
predictions, and GB-LCSS refines targets. Its advantage over Graph-MLP is dataset
dependent (Table 4), while the distillation experiment shows that the same targets can
also benefit an MLP student through a GCN teacher (Table 8).
Granular balls provide a practical organization for local-consensus supervision.

Each node belongs to one ball, whose class counts are shared when constructing targets
for its training nodes. Geometric splitting adapts the partition to the representation while
limiting the number of balls and enforcing a minimum size for each child ball. Granular
ball grouping requires less storage than exact kNN on the three larger graphs and has
the shortest construction time on three datasets (Fig. 5). These results support its use as
a grouping mechanism that combines competitive accuracy, shared regional statistics,
and controlled construction costs.
5. Conclusion
We presented GB-LCSS for learning from noisy training labels when the deployed
classifier receives only node attributes. The method organizes nodes into granular balls
in a frozen, label-free graph representation and combines each observed label with
the consensus of the other training nodes in its ball. Our analysis relates local-vote
reliability to the number of available votes and their clean-class margin under symmetric
noise, decomposes the squared error of the soft target, and gives a sufficient condition
for it to have lower expected error than the observed label.
Across five datasets and five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves the highest five-seed
mean accuracy in 24 of the 25 settings, compared with seven noisy-label baselines that
use the same deployed MLP architecture. Its soft targets have lower mean Brier scores
than the observed one-hot labels in all 25 settings. The controlled comparisons support
the importance of which training nodes contribute to the local consensus, with granular
balls providing a compact way to share label statistics. The distillation experiments
further show that these targets can improve the MLP student when they are used to train
its graph teacher.
The present evaluation focuses on synthetic label noise in a fixed-graph transductive
setting, with clean validation labels used for model selection. Future work will test the
method on naturally occurring annotation errors and develop model-selection procedures
for imperfect validation labels. Another direction is to evaluate generalization to newly
arriving nodes and to investigate how the representations and granular ball partitions
can be updated as the training graph grows.
