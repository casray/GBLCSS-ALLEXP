1. Introduction
Node classification is a fundamental task in graph learning, where node attributes and relationships jointly support
prediction. Graph neural networks (GNNs) combine these sources of information by aggregating representations from
neighboring nodes [1, 2]. Their dependence on graph structure, however, creates practical difficulties at deployment.
Predictions require access to the relevant neighborhoods, while retrieving and aggregating multi-hop neighbor informa-
tion adds computational cost. In applications with missing relations, privacy restrictions, or low-latency requirements,
thisaccessmaybeunavailableorimpractical. Theseconstraintshavemotivatedgraph-freenodeclassificationmethods
such as Graph-MLP [3] and GLNN [4], which exploit graph structure during training but use only node attributes at
inference.
Subsequent work has explored richer forms of graph-to-MLP knowledge transfer. SimMLP aligned GNN and MLP
representations without supervision [5], while FF-G2M transferred low- and high-frequency information from a GNN
teacher [6]. These developments broaden the ways in which graph structure can guide MLP training.
Noisy training labels pose an additional problem. Labels obtained through manual annotation, automated rules, or
multiple sources may contain errors, and deep models can eventually fit corrupted annotations [7]. On graphs, message
passing may propagate the influence of mislabeled nodes to their neighbors [8]. Many existing noise-robust graph

methods use topology to augment supervision [9], regulate training with noisy labels [10], or select reliable samples
[11]. These methods address label noise but still require graph access at inference. Our aim is to use graph structure
to improve noisy-label supervision during training while allowing the classifier to predict from node attributes alone.
One way to address this challenge is to combine an observed label with evidence from nearby training nodes.
When nearby training nodes exhibit consistent class evidence, aggregating their labels can reduce the influence of
isolated annotation errors. Neighborhood-based methods for noisy-label learning have exploited related forms of local
consistency [12, 13]. For graph data, a graph contrastive representation learned from node attributes and connectivity
without labels provides a basis for identifying such local groups [14]. These groups can then supply label evidence
without allowing noisy annotations to determine their membership.
Granular ball computing provides a regional representation for this purpose. It groups samples into coarse-grained
units summarized by centers and radii [15]. Subsequent studies have investigated granular ball sampling for noisy or
imbalancedclassification[16]andrefinedadaptiveballgeneration[17]. Dependingonthetask,ballconstructioncanbe
guided by label purity or geometric criteria [18]. Geometry-based construction is suitable here because it allows local
groups to be formed before the training labels are aggregated. Each group can then provide shared label statistics for
constructing supervision for its members.
Buildingonthisidea,weproposeGranularBallLocal-ConsensusSoftSupervision(GB-LCSS)fortransductivenode
classificationwith noisy labels and graph-freeinference. As showninFig. 1, GB-LCSSfirst learnsnode representations
through label-free graph contrastive learning. It then recursively applies 2-means to node representations in the frozen
representation space, assigning each node to exactly one granular ball. For each training node, the labels of the other
trainingnodesinitsballprovidealocalconsensus. Aweightdeterminedbytheagreementamongtheselabelscombines
thisconsensuswiththenode’sobservedlabeltoformasofttarget. ThesetargetsareusedtotrainanMLPthatreceives
only node attributes at inference.
ExperimentsonfivedatasetsandfivenoisesettingsshowthatGB-LCSSattainsortiesforthehighestfive-seedmean
accuracy in 24 of 25 settings among seven noisy-label baselines evaluated using the same deployed MLP architecture.
Controlled comparisons examine how the labels of training nodes within each region contribute to target construction,
and a graph-teacher extension evaluates the same targets for distillation.
The main contributions are summarized as follows:
(1) We organize nodes into granular balls in a frozen, label-free graph representation space, assigning each node to one
local group. This separates region construction from noisy-label aggregation.
(2) We develop consensus-weighted soft supervision within these regions. Each training node combines its observed
label with the labels of the other training nodes in its ball, with their agreement determining the contribution of
local consensus. We analyze the reliability of local voting under independent symmetric label noise and derive an
exact decomposition of the squared probability error of the resulting soft target.
(3) We evaluate GB-LCSS on five datasets and five noise settings against noisy-label and graph-assisted baselines.
Controlled studies examine regional membership, target construction, and representation learning, and a graph-
teacher extension evaluates the same targets for distillation.

2.1. Learning with noisy labels
Methodsforlearningwithnoisylabelsaimtoreducetheinfluenceofcorruptedannotationsthroughsampleselection,
target refinement, or robust optimization. Sample selection exploits the tendency of deep networks to learn clean
patterns before memorizing noise. Co-teaching [19], for example, exchanged small-loss samples between two networks,
while JoCoR [20] also encouraged prediction agreement. DivideMix [21] treated clean and noisy samples within a
semi-supervised framework. Beyond loss-based selection, FINE [22] assessed class-wise feature directions, and CT [23]
trackedprediction confidence. Knockoffs-SPR[24] used penalized regression and knockofffilters to select clean samples
while controlling false selections under its theoretical assumptions. Label agreement among nearest neighbors in a
feature space has also been used directly: Deep k-NN [13] filtered samples whose labels disagree with those of their
neighbors, and neighbor-consistency regularization [12] encouraged each prediction to match those of its neighbors.
Other methods address label noise by modifying the training loss. SCE [25] combined cross-entropy with reverse
cross-entropy, while ELR [26] regularized training to limit noise memorization. ANL [27] used active negative loss
functions, and LogitClip [28] constrained logit norms. These strategies address annotation noise without explicitly
modeling graph relations between samples.
2.2. Noise-robust learning on graphs
Graphstructurecanprovideadditionalevidenceforassessinglabelreliability,whilemessagepassingmayalsospread
the influence of supervision errors [8]. UnionNET [29] used neighborhood-based label estimates for reweighting and
3
correction, NRGNN [9] augmented supervision through structural information and pseudo-labeling, and RTGNN [10]
coordinated two networks to regulate noisy supervision.
Labelreliabilitycanalsobeassessedthroughgraphtopologyandmodelbehavior. TSS[11]usedtopologicalsample
selection,whileTFR[30]reconstructedtopologicalfeatures. ProCon[31]usedpeer-informedlabelconsistency,andGD2
[32] identified unreliable supervision through prediction discrepancies between two views. GNN-CFGD [33] combined
coarse- and fine-grained sample division to address label noise and sparsity.
Beyond assessing label reliability, several methods have constructed additional supervision. DND-NET [34] used
neighborhood uncertainty to reweight pseudo-label supervision, whereas RNCGLN [35] combined graph contrastive
learning with pseudo graphs and pseudo labels to address graph and label noise. Prototype-Guided Supervision [36]
formed class-level prototypes by aggregating representations of nodes with the same observed label. BO-NNC [37] and
SAMTD [38] improved supervision through multiple graph teachers.
Other methods address label noise by modifying graph structure, neighborhood aggregation, or the training ob-
jective. GTA [39] adapted graph topology, MILAB [40] combined information bottleneck-guided aggregation with
adversarialconsistency, andHALF[41]fusedlow-andhigh-passlossesaccordingtolocalhomophilytoidentifyreliable
labels. NoisyGL [8] established a unified evaluation framework for learning with noisy labels on graphs.
2.3. Graph-free node classification
Methods for graph-free node classification exploit graph information during training while avoiding online message
passing at inference. Graph-MLP [3] incorporated adjacency through a neighborhood-contrastive objective, whereas
GLNN [4] distilled predictions from a GNN teacher into an MLP. Both methods deploy an MLP that receives only
node attributes. GSDN [42] used neighborhood self-distillation, SimMLP [5] aligned GNN and MLP representations
without supervision, and ES-MLP [43] incorporated edge splitting into contrastive training. Tian et al. [44] analyzed
the effectiveness, robustness, and efficiency of graph-trained MLPs, while SA-MLP [45] investigated structure-aware
distillation. Thesemethodsdifferinhowgraphinformationguidestrainingandwhetherprecomputedstructuralfeatures
are retained at inference.
Subsequent distillation methods differ in the teacher information they transfer. FF-G2M [6] separated low- and
high-frequency knowledge, KRD [46] assessed knowledge reliability through the stability of prediction entropy under
noiseperturbations,andVQGraph[47]encodedlocalstructuresasdiscretetokens. PGKD[48]usedclassprototypesto
guidestructuralknowledgedistillationfromGNNstoMLPs,whereasTINED[49]combinedteacherparameterinjection
with Dirichlet-energy distillation. AdaGMLP [50], ProGMLP [51], and SALE-MLP [52] investigated adaptive transfer,
accuracy–cost trade-offs, and structure-aware latent representations, respectively. Yu et al. [53] theoretically analyzed
how label alignment in distillation allows an MLP to learn a GNN teacher’s implicit graph bias.
A related line of work uses graph structure to construct soft training targets. PosteL [54] constructed posterior soft
labels from neighborhood labels and graph-wide label statistics. ALS [55] used neighborhood label distributions and
adaptively updated smoothed targets during graph training, while SALS [56] incorporated structural information into
thetargetlabeldistribution. Thesemethodsdrawongraphneighborhoodsandlabelstatisticstoconstructtheirtargets.
GB-LCSS also modifies the training targets, but defines regions by proximity in a frozen, label-free representation and
excludes each node’s own label from the consensus used to adjust its target.
2.4. Graph contrastive representation learning
Self-supervisedgraphlearningderivessupervisionfromnodeattributesandgraphconnectivityratherthanannotated
classlabels[57]. Graphcontrastivelearningconstructsdifferentviewsofagraphbyperturbingitsstructureorattributes.
Acommonnode-levelobjectivebringsrepresentationsofthesamenodeacrossviewsclosertogetherwhiledistinguishing
them from those of other nodes. The resulting node vectors provide a basis for comparing nodes without using their
observed class labels.
4
GRACE [14] implements this approach through random edge dropping and feature masking, with a shared graph
encoder processing the two views. GB-LCSS adopts this node-level contrastive objective to learn the representations
used for granular ball construction. After pretraining, the encoder is frozen and nodes are grouped according to their
representations. Theobservedtraininglabelsareusedonlyafterwardstoconstructsoftsupervision. Thisseparatesthe
information used to form local groups from the potentially noisy labels aggregated within them.
2.5. Granular ball computing
Granular ball computing represents samples using coarse-grained hyperspherical regions, which GBkNN used to
classify queries [15]. Granular ball sampling extended this representation to sample selection for noisy or imbalanced
classification [16]. MGNR [58] used granular ball representations to construct multi-granularity neighborhood relation-
ships for classification and clustering. For noisy-label learning, MgCNL [59] used multi-granularity balls for sample
separation, while ball-intra fusion [60] combined information within granular regions. VPGB [61] studied attribute
reduction and classification with noisy labels, and GEAF [62] filtered label noise through adaptive granular-ellipsoid
partitioning.
Granularrepresentationshavealsobeenincorporatedintodeeplearning. GBRAIN[63], G-GBC[64], andBNO-GB
[65]appliedgranularregionstorobusttextclassification,CNNclassification,andopenintentclassification,respectively.
Ongraphs,supervisedgranularballcoarsening[66]andGBGC[67]usedgranularregionstoconstructcoarsenedgraphs,
while MGGR [68] explored multi-granularity graph refinement for graph classification.
Insupervisedgranularballclassificationandsampling,labelpuritycanguidewhetheraballshouldbesplit[15–17].
Label-freegeometriccriteriaarealsousedingranularballcomputing[18]. GB-LCSSusesthelattertypeofcriterionin
a frozen graph representation: the partition is formed without observed labels, and each region then determines which
training nodes contribute to the local consensus. The balls organize supervision during training, while the deployed
MLP predicts from node attributes.
Theselinesofworkmotivatecombininglabel-freegraphrepresentationswithlabelaggregationwithinlocalregions.
GB-LCSS uses this combination to improve noisy supervision for an MLP while retaining the graph-free inference
interface of methods such as Graph-MLP and GLNN.

4.1.3. Compared methods
Thefirstcomparisongroupcomprisessevennoisy-labelbaselines: MLP+CE,Small-loss, Co-teaching, JoCoR,SCE,
ELR, and LogitClip. They use the same deployed two-layer MLP and labeled training nodes as GB-LCSS. GB-LCSS
additionally uses graph structure during training. Small-loss, Co-teaching, and JoCoR receive the known synthetic
noise rate for their forget-rate schedules; the other methods in this group and GB-LCSS do not. Small-loss selects
samples by per-sample cross-entropy. Co-teaching and JoCoR train two independently initialized MLPs but evaluate
one network without ensembling.
The second group includes PosteL [54], ALS [55], SALS [56], Graph-MLP [3], and GSDN [42], which use graph
information during training and receive only node attributes at inference in our protocol. PosteL integrates the official
target-construction code with iterative pseudo-labeling disabled. Graph-MLP is ported to the common protocol, while
ALS, SALS, and GSDN use mechanism adaptations. Appendix F specifies the implementation basis, adaptations, and
parameter sources.
12
The third group compares graph-to-MLP transfer methods: a GCN(X)+CE response-distillation baseline based on
GLNN [4], FF-G2M [6], KRD [46], VQGraph [47], and a GCN teacher trained on GB-LCSS targets (Section 3.5). FF-
G2M is reimplemented with common backbones and its low- and high-frequency objectives. The KRD and VQGraph
mechanism adaptations retain reliable-knowledge selection and soft-codebook mechanisms, respectively. For each seed,
we retrain the teacher and student for every route using a common MLP configuration.
4.1.4. Implementation details
Datasets are loaded through the NoisyGL interface to PyTorch Geometric. Graph contrastive pretraining uses
row-normalized attributes, whereas downstream models use the attributes supplied by NoisyGL. Graph-convolutional
models use the supplied adjacency, with normalization and self-loops handled within their graph-convolution layers.
The noisy-label baselines and GB-LCSS use a two-layer MLP with hidden width 64 and dropout 0.5. We train for
200 epochs using Adam with a learning rate of 0.01 and weight decay 5×10−4, and select the checkpoint with the
highest validation accuracy. Teacher checkpoints use the same selection criterion.
GB-LCSS uses B = 64, n = 32, δ = 0.05, s = 2, and β = 0.5 as the default settings. Recursive 2-means
max min min
uses Euclidean distance, k-means++ initialization [71], and 10 initializations per candidate split, with randomization
controlled by the downstream seed. The graph encoder uses the fixed GRACE configurations listed in Appendix F and
is frozen after pretraining. The distillation extension uses temperature τ =1. Complete parameters and their sources
appear in Appendix F.
4.1.5. Evaluation protocol
Unless stated otherwise, test-node classification accuracy is reported as percentages, with mean ± sample standard
deviation over five paired seeds. Bold marks the highest mean within each dataset and noise setting; in Table 4,
underlining marks the second-highest mean. Each dataset uses one fixed pretrained representation H; the seeds vary
the split, noise realization, downstream initialization, and 2-means initialization. Section 4.3.3 separately examines
variation across GRACE pretraining seeds.
We assess same-seed accuracy differences using two-sided paired t-tests and 95% confidence intervals. To account
for multiple comparisons, Holm correction [72] is applied separately to each set of 25 tests against the following ref-
erence methods: random size-matched grouping, MLP+CE, and JoCoR. Appendix B gives the full paired results and
calculation details.

5. Conclusion
Learning from noisy labels remains a challenge for node classifiers that must operate without graph access at
inference. GB-LCSS addresses this problem by constructing granular balls in a frozen, label-free graph representation
and combining regional label consensus with each node’s observed label. This separates region construction from
noisy-label aggregation and provides soft targets for an MLP that uses only node attributes at inference. The analysis
characterizes local-vote reliability under independent symmetric noise and decomposes the squared probability error of
the resulting soft target.
Across five datasets and five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves or ties for the highest five-seed mean accuracy in
24 of 25 settings among seven noisy-label baselines evaluated using the same deployed MLP architecture. Controlled
comparisons support representation-based regional supervision and generally favor excluding the current node’s vote
when forming consensus. The resulting soft targets have lower Brier scores than the observed one-hot targets, and the
graph-teacher extension improves mean student accuracy over direct MLP training in all ten evaluated settings.
The current evaluation uses synthetic label noise and clean validation labels in a fixed-graph transductive setting.
Futureworkwillexaminerealannotationerrors,modelselectionwithoutcleanvalidationlabels,andinductiveprediction
for newly arriving nodes.
