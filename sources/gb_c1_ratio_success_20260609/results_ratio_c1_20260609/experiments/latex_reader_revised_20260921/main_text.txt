Abstract
Graph-freenodeclassificationenablespredictionfromnodeattributeswhengraphrelationsareunavailableorcostlyto
access at deployment. However, existing noise-robust graph methods generally retain graph-dependent inference, while
graph-free methods have largely been studied with clean training labels. To address this gap, we propose Granular
Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision (GB-LCSS) for transductive node classification with noisy labels and graph-
free inference. GB-LCSS learns node representations through label-free graph contrastive learning, freezes them, and
recursively groups nodes into granular balls. For each training node, the observed labels of the other training nodes in
itsballformalocalconsensus. Aweightdeterminedbytheiragreementblendsthisconsensuswiththenode’sobserved
label to produce a soft target for a multilayer perceptron (MLP). This separates region construction from noisy-label
aggregation and uses local evidence to reduce the influence of isolated annotation errors. Across five datasets and
five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves or ties for the highest five-seed mean accuracy in 24 of 25 settings among seven
noisy-label baselines using the same deployed MLP architecture. Its targets also have lower mean Brier scores than the
observed one-hot labels in all 25 settings, while the deployed classifier requires only node attributes.
Keywords: label noise, node classification, graph-free inference, graph contrastive learning, granular ball computing,
soft labels
1. Introduction
Node classification is a fundamental task in graph learning, where node attributes and relationships jointly support
prediction. Graph neural networks (GNNs) combine these sources of information by aggregating representations from
neighboring nodes [1, 2]. Their dependence on graph structure, however, creates practical difficulties at deployment.
Predictions require access to the relevant neighborhoods, while retrieving and aggregating multi-hop neighbor informa-
tion adds computational cost. In applications with missing relations, privacy restrictions, or low-latency requirements,
thisaccessmaybeunavailableorimpractical. Theseconstraintshavemotivatedgraph-freenodeclassification,inwhich
graph structure is used during training and the deployed classifier receives only node attributes. Graph-MLP [3] and
GLNN [4] demonstrated this approach, and later work such as SimMLP [5] and FF-G2M [6] refined which structural
knowledge is transferred to the MLP and how. This line of work has largely focused on structural knowledge transfer
with clean labels for classifier training and evaluation.
Noisy training labels pose an additional problem. Labels obtained through manual annotation, automated rules, or
multiple sources may contain errors, and deep models can fit corrupted annotations [7]. On graphs, message passing
may propagate the influence of mislabeled nodes to their neighbors [8]. Noise-robust graph methods use topology to
augment supervision [9], to regulate training with noisy labels [10], or to select reliable samples [11]. These methods

One way to address this challenge is to combine an observed label with evidence from nearby training nodes.
When their labels provide consistent evidence for the underlying class, aggregating them can reduce the influence of
isolated annotation errors. Neighborhood-based methods for noisy-label learning have exploited related forms of local
consistency [12, 13]. For graph data, a graph contrastive representation learned from node attributes and connectivity
without class labels provides a basis for identifying such local groups [14]. The groups can then supply label evidence
while their membership is determined independently of the observed labels.
Granular ball computing provides a regional representation for this purpose. It groups samples into coarse-grained
units summarized by their centers and radii [15, 16]. Subsequent studies have investigated granular ball sampling for
noisyorimbalancedclassification[17]andrefinedadaptiveballgeneration[18]. Dependingonthetask,ballconstruction

constructing its members’ supervision.
Buildingonthisidea,weproposeGranularBallLocal-ConsensusSoftSupervision(GB-LCSS)fortransductivenode

the other training nodes in its ball provide a local consensus, and a weight determined by the agreement among these
labels blends the consensus with the node’s observed label to form a soft target. These targets train an MLP that
receives only node attributes at inference.

2.2. Noise-robust learning on graphs
Graphstructurecanprovideadditionalevidenceforassessinglabelreliability,whilemessagepassingmayalsospread
the influence of supervision errors [8]. UnionNET [29] used neighborhood-based label estimates for reweighting and
correction, NRGNN [9] augmented supervision through structural information and pseudo-labeling, and RTGNN [10]
coordinated two networks to regulate noisy supervision.
Labelreliabilitycanalsobeassessedthroughgraphtopologyandmodelbehavior. TSS[11]usedtopologicalsample
selection,whileTFR[30]reconstructedtopologicalfeatures. ProCon[31]usedpeer-informedlabelconsistency,andGD2
[32] identified unreliable supervision through prediction discrepancies between two views. GNN-CFGD [33] combined
coarse- and fine-grained sample division to address label noise and sparsity.
Beyond assessing label reliability, several methods have constructed additional supervision. DND-NET [34] used
neighborhood uncertainty to reweight pseudo-label supervision, whereas RNCGLN [35] combined graph contrastive
3
learning with pseudo graphs and pseudo labels to address graph and label noise. Prototype-Guided Supervision [36]
formed class-level prototypes by aggregating representations of nodes with the same observed label. BO-NNC [37] and
SAMTD [38] improved supervision through multiple graph teachers.
Other methods address label noise by modifying graph structure, neighborhood aggregation, or the training ob-
jective. GTA [39] adapted graph topology, MILAB [40] combined information bottleneck-guided aggregation with
adversarialconsistency, andHALF[41]fusedlow-andhigh-passlossesaccordingtolocalhomophilytoidentifyreliable
labels. NoisyGL [8] established a unified evaluation framework for learning with noisy labels on graphs.
2.3. Graph-free node classification
Methods for graph-free node classification exploit graph information during training while avoiding online message
passing at inference. Graph-MLP [3] incorporated adjacency through a neighborhood-contrastive objective, whereas
GLNN [4] distilled predictions from a GNN teacher into an MLP. Both methods deploy an MLP that receives only
node attributes. GSDN [42] used neighborhood self-distillation, SimMLP [5] aligned GNN and MLP representations
without supervision, and ES-MLP [43] incorporated edge splitting into contrastive training. Tian et al. [44] analyzed
the effectiveness, robustness, and efficiency of graph-trained MLPs, while SA-MLP [45] investigated structure-aware
distillation. Thesemethodsdifferinhowgraphinformationguidestrainingandwhetherprecomputedstructuralfeatures
are retained at inference.
Subsequent distillation methods differ in the teacher information they transfer. FF-G2M [6] separated low- and
high-frequency knowledge, KRD [46] assessed knowledge reliability through the stability of prediction entropy under
noiseperturbations,andVQGraph[47]encodedlocalstructuresasdiscretetokens. PGKD[48]usedclassprototypesto
guidestructuralknowledgedistillationfromGNNstoMLPs,whereasTINED[49]combinedteacherparameterinjection
with Dirichlet-energy distillation. AdaGMLP [50], ProGMLP [51], and SALE-MLP [52] investigated adaptive transfer,
accuracy–cost trade-offs, and structure-aware latent representations, respectively. Yu et al. [53] theoretically analyzed
how label alignment in distillation allows an MLP to learn a GNN teacher’s implicit graph bias.
A related line of work uses graph structure to construct soft training targets. PosteL [54] constructed posterior soft
labels from neighborhood labels and graph-wide label statistics. ALS [55] used neighborhood label distributions and
adaptively updated smoothed targets during graph training, while SALS [56] incorporated structural information into
the target label distribution. These methods define the neighborhood of a node by graph adjacency and smooth its
label toward the labels found there. GB-LCSS also modifies the training targets, but defines regions by proximity in a
frozen, label-free representation and excludes each node’s own label from the consensus that adjusts it.
2.4. Graph contrastive representation learning
Self-supervised graph learning constructs training signals from node attributes and graph connectivity [57]. Graph
contrastivelearningconstructsdifferentviewsofagraphbyperturbingitsstructureorattributes. Acommonnode-level
objective brings representations of the same node across views closer together while distinguishing them from those of
other nodes. The resulting node vectors provide a basis for comparing nodes without using their observed class labels.
GRACE [14] implements this approach through random edge dropping and feature masking, with a shared graph
encoder processing the two views. Its contrastive objective uses no class labels, and the learned node vectors can be
frozen for subsequent grouping. GB-LCSS adopts these representations to construct granular balls; observed training
labels are then aggregated within the groups to form soft targets.
2.5. Granular ball computing
Granular ball computing represents samples using coarse-grained hyperspherical regions, which GBkNN used to
classify queries [15]. Granular ball sampling extended this representation to sample selection for noisy or imbalanced
classification [17]. MGNR [58] used granular ball representations to construct multi-granularity neighborhood relation-
ships for classification and clustering. For noisy-label learning, MgCNL [59] used multi-granularity balls for sample
4
separation, while ball-intra fusion [60] combined information within granular regions. VPGB [61] studied attribute
reduction and classification with noisy labels, and GEAF [62] filtered label noise through adaptive granular-ellipsoid
partitioning.
Granularrepresentationshavealsobeenincorporatedintodeeplearning. GBRAIN[63], G-GBC[64], andBNO-GB
[65]appliedgranularregionstorobusttextclassification,CNNclassification,andopenintentclassification,respectively.
Ongraphs,supervisedgranularballcoarsening[66]andGBGC[67]usedgranularregionstoconstructcoarsenedgraphs,
while MGGR [68] explored multi-granularity graph refinement for graph classification.
In supervised granular ball classifiers and sampling methods, label purity can guide ball generation [15, 17, 18].
Geometricsplittingcriteriathatusenolabelshavealsobeendevelopedforclustering[16]. GB-LCSSappliesageometric
criterion in a frozen graph representation and uses the resulting groups to define which training nodes contribute to
each other’s soft targets.
These studies provide complementary ways to use graph structure and local label evidence. GB-LCSS brings them
together by constructing label-free regions and using their observed training labels to refine supervision for an MLP
with graph-free inference.

4.1.2. Data splits and label noise
All comparisons use a fixed-graph transductive protocol. Complete node attributes and adjacency, including those
of test nodes, are available as unlabeled context during representation learning and partitioning. For each seed from
0 to 4, we use the NoisyGL percentage-split routine to randomly sample training, validation, and test nodes from the
full node set at ratios of 20%, 10%, and 70%. Methods share the same split and noisy training labels for each dataset
and seed. Noise is injected into training labels only. Clean validation labels are used for checkpoint selection, and test
labels are used for final evaluation. Appendix F gives the exact subset sizes and sampling procedure, and Appendix C
examines corrupted validation labels.
Uniform noise uses P = 1−ρ and P = ρ/(C −1), with ρ ∈ {0.2,0.4,0.6}. Pair noise uses ρ = 0.4 and changes
ii ij
class i to ((i−2)modC)+1 with probability ρ, leaving it unchanged with probability 1−ρ. Instance-dependent noise
follows the NoisyGL generator with a configured mean flip rate of 0.4 and norm std=0.1.
4.1.3. Compared methods
Thefirstcomparisongroupcomprisessevennoisy-labelbaselines: MLP+CE,Small-loss, Co-teaching, JoCoR,SCE,
ELR, and LogitClip. They use the same deployed two-layer MLP and the same labeled training nodes as GB-LCSS,
so the comparison matches the classifier architecture and the label budget while the training-time resources differ:
GB-LCSS reads the graph during training, and Small-loss, Co-teaching, and JoCoR are given the true noise rate to set
their forget-rate schedules, which GB-LCSS does not require. Small-loss selects samples by per-sample cross-entropy.
Co-teaching and JoCoR train two independently initialized MLPs but evaluate one network without ensembling.
The second group includes PosteL [54], ALS [55], SALS [56], Graph-MLP [3], and GSDN [42], which use graph
information during training and receive only node attributes at inference in our protocol. PosteL integrates the official
target-construction code with iterative pseudo-labeling disabled. Graph-MLP is ported to the common protocol, while
ALS, SALS, and GSDN use mechanism adaptations. Appendix F specifies the implementation basis, adaptations, and
parameter sources.
The third group compares graph-to-MLP transfer methods: a GCN(X)+CE response-distillation baseline based on
GLNN [4], FF-G2M [6], KRD [46], VQGraph [47], and a GCN teacher trained on GB-LCSS targets (Section 3.5). FF-
G2M is reimplemented with common backbones and its low- and high-frequency objectives. The KRD and VQGraph
mechanism adaptations retain reliable-knowledge selection and soft-codebook mechanisms, respectively. For each seed,
we retrain the teacher and student for every route using a common MLP configuration.
4.1.4. Implementation details
Datasets are loaded through the NoisyGL interface to PyTorch Geometric. Graph contrastive pretraining uses
row-normalized attributes, whereas downstream models use the attributes supplied by NoisyGL. Graph-convolutional
models use the supplied adjacency, with normalization and self-loops handled within their graph-convolution layers.
The noisy-label baselines and GB-LCSS use a two-layer MLP with hidden width 64 and dropout 0.5. We train for
200 epochs using Adam with a learning rate of 0.01 and weight decay 5×10−4, and select the checkpoint with the
highest validation accuracy. Teacher checkpoints use the same selection criterion.
GB-LCSS uses B = 64, n = 32, δ = 0.05, s = 2, and β = 0.5 as the default settings. Recursive 2-means
max min min
uses Euclidean distance, k-means++ initialization [71], and 10 initializations per candidate split, with randomization
controlled by the downstream seed. The graph encoder uses the fixed GRACE configurations listed in Appendix F and
is frozen after pretraining. The distillation extension uses temperature τ =1. Complete parameters and their sources
appear in Appendix F.
4.1.5. Evaluation protocol
Test-node classification accuracy is reported in percent as mean ± sample standard deviation over five paired
seeds. Bold marks the highest mean within each dataset and noise setting; in Table 4, underlining marks the second-
highest mean. Each dataset uses one fixed pretrained representation H; the seeds vary the split, the noise realization,
12
the downstream initialization, and the 2-means initialization, so the reported variability is conditional on the fixed
representation. Section 4.3.3 separately examines variation across GRACE pretraining seeds.
Same-seed accuracy differences are assessed with two-sided paired t-tests and 95% confidence intervals. Holm
correction [72] is applied separately to each family of 25 tests against random size-matched grouping, MLP+CE, and
JoCoR. Appendix B reports the paired comparisons, confidence intervals, and Holm-adjusted p-values.

4.5. Discussion
GB-LCSS improves supervision by combining an individual annotation with evidence from related training nodes.
The representation determines which nodes provide this evidence, and the agreement among their labels determines
its weight. Label-free grouping keeps corrupted annotations from shaping the regions, while excluding the current
node makes its consensus depend on other training nodes. The random-grouping and smoothing controls show that
preservinglocalmembershipimprovesaccuracy(Table5). Proposition1explainshowvotecountandclassmarginaffect
local-vote reliability under independent symmetric noise. The target-error decomposition provides a complementary
empirical view: under uniform 0.4, the reduction on corrupted labels exceeds the increase on initially correct labels in
all 25 dataset–seed runs (Appendix I.2).
The quality of local evidence depends on the labels within each group. Agreement on the underlying class can
counter isolated annotation errors, while an incorrect local majority can bias the target. When all observed training
labelsinaballagree,thetargetretainsthatlabel. Theclean-labelcontrolsandadditional-datasetresults(AppendicesD
and G) show that the benefit relative to other training strategies varies across datasets. The consistent advantage over
randomsize-matchedgroupingindicatesthatpreservinglocalrelationshipsremainsusefulfortargetconstructionacross
the evaluated graphs.
GB-LCSS shares the attribute-only inference interface of Graph-MLP and GLNN. The distinction is how graph
information is used during training: GB-LCSS constructs local-consensus targets, Graph-MLP adds a neighborhood-
contrastive objective, and GLNN transfers a GNN teacher’s predictions. The teacher extension shows that local-
consensus targets can also support graph-to-MLP distillation. Replacing observed labels with these targets for GCN
teacher training improves student accuracy in nine of ten settings. This extension expands supervision to all nodes
through teacher predictions and preserves the deployed MLP.
Granularballsprovideapracticalorganizationforthissupervision. Eachnodeisassignedtoexactlyonegroup,and
regional class statistics are reused across its node-specific targets. Geometric splitting adapts the number of regions to
the representation within the prescribed budget and minimum-size constraint. The grouping comparison shows similar
accuracytokNNandk-meansonfourdatasets,whilethecostmeasurementsshowlowerstoragethanexactkNNonthe
threelarger graphs and theshortest construction time onthree datasets. Together, theseresults support granularballs
19
as a compact, reusable regional structure with competitive predictive performance. The parameter analysis further
identifies local grouping and broad coverage of consensus supervision as important aspects of this construction.
5. Conclusion
Learning from noisy labels remains a challenge for node classifiers that must operate without graph access at
inference. GB-LCSS addresses this problem by organizing nodes into granular balls in a frozen, label-free graph
representation and combining regional label consensus with each node’s observed label. The resulting soft targets train
anMLPthatusesonlynodeattributesatinference. Ouranalysischaracterizeslocal-votereliabilityunderindependent
symmetric noise and decomposes the squared probability error of the soft target.
Across five datasets and five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves or ties for the highest five-seed mean accuracy in 24
of 25 settings among seven noisy-label baselines using the same deployed MLP architecture. Its soft targets have lower
mean Brier scores than the observed one-hot labels in all 25 settings. Controlled comparisons demonstrate the value of
representation-basedgrouping, andthegraph-teacherextensionimprovesstudentaccuracyoverdirectMLPtrainingin
all ten evaluated settings. Granular balls provide shared regional statistics and a controlled number of groups for this
supervision.
The experiments evaluate GB-LCSS with synthetic label noise and clean validation labels on fixed graphs. Future
work will examine real annotation errors and develop model-selection procedures for settings where clean validation
labels are unavailable. Extending local-consensus construction to newly arriving nodes would further broaden the
method’s applicability beyond the current transductive setting.
