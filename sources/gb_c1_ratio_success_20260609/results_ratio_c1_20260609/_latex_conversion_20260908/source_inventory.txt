2 Header: Abstract
3 Para: In many deployment scenarios, graph structure is available during training, whereas inference must rely only on node attributes because relations are missing or online graph processing is constrained. Methods for learning with noisy labels on graphs generally retain graph-dependent inference, while graph-free node classification has largely been studied with clean supervision. We propose Granular-Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision (GB-LCSS) for transductive node classification with noisy labels and graph-free inference. GB-LCSS first learns label-free node representations from attributes and graph connectivity and recursively partitions the resulting space into a bounded set of disjoint granular balls. For each training node, GB-LCSS aggregates the labels of the other training nodes in the same ball and adaptively blends their consensus with the node's observed label to form a soft target. Label-free region construction prevents noisy annotations from shaping the partition, while the adaptive target reduces the influence of isolated label errors when local evidence is consistent. Across five datasets and five noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves the highest or tied-highest five-seed mean accuracy in 24 of 25 settings among seven noisy-label baselines using the same deployed MLP architecture, while requiring only node attributes at inference.
4 Para: Keywords: label noise, node classification, graph-free inference, graph contrastive learning, granular-ball computing, knowledge distillation
5 Header: 1 Introduction
6 Para: Graph neural networks (GNNs) are effective for node classification because message passing combines node attributes with relational context [1,2]. This dependency also complicates deployment: prediction requires access to the graph and repeated neighborhood aggregation, which may be costly or infeasible when relations are unavailable or online graph processing is restricted. Graph-free approaches such as Graph-MLP and GLNN exploit graph structure during training but deploy an MLP that predicts directly from node attributes [3,4]. This removes graph access and neighborhood aggregation from online prediction.
7 Para: Noisy training labels introduce a second challenge. Labels collected through manual annotation, automated rules, or multiple sources can contain errors, and deep models may eventually memorize corrupted supervision [8]. On graphs, message passing can further spread the effect of mislabeled nodes [13]. Noise-robust graph methods use topology for label correction, pseudo-labeling, sample selection, or robust representation learning [9–12,47], but their predictions generally remain graph dependent. Existing graph-free studies, by contrast, have largely focused on structural knowledge transfer with clean supervision. Robustness to noisy training labels remains insufficiently studied in graph-free node classification.
8 Para: To improve supervision without introducing graph dependence at inference, GB-LCSS uses the training graph to construct soft targets for the deployed MLP. The method first learns label-free node representations with a graph contrastive encoder instantiated by GRACE [16], so label noise does not alter the geometry used for partitioning. It then recursively divides this representation space into a bounded set of disjoint granular balls [34]. For each training node, GB-LCSS counts the observed labels of the other training nodes in the same ball to form a local consensus. Greater agreement among these labels gives the consensus more weight, helping counter isolated label errors, whereas weak agreement retains more of the node's observed label. The resulting node-specific soft targets train an MLP that receives only node attributes at inference.
9 Para: We evaluate GB-LCSS on Cora, CiteSeer, CoraFull, Coauthor-CS, and Amazon-Photo under uniform, pair, and instance-dependent label noise. Across five noise settings, it achieves the highest or tied-highest five-seed mean accuracy in 24 of 25 settings among seven noise-robust baselines using the same deployed MLP architecture. A secondary graph-teacher extension trains on the same consensus targets and supplies predictions over all nodes, improving the MLP student's mean accuracy without changing its inference inputs.

12 Header: 2 Related work
13 Header: 2.1 Learning with noisy labels
14 Para: Learning with noisy labels reduces the influence of corrupted annotations by controlling which samples are trusted, how supervision is represented, or how strongly a model fits uncertain targets. Sample-selection methods exploit the tendency of deep networks to fit clean patterns before memorizing noise. Co-teaching exchanges small-loss samples between two networks, while JoCoR also enforces agreement between their predictions [17,18]. DivideMix treats the separated clean and noisy samples as a semi-supervised learning problem, whereas FINE and CT estimate sample reliability from class-wise feature directions and confidence trajectories [23–25].
15 Para: A complementary family modifies the training objective. SCE combines cross-entropy with reverse cross-entropy, ELR suppresses late-stage memorization, and ANL and LogitClip reduce sensitivity to corrupted targets through negative learning and logit-norm control [19–22]. These methods provide general strategies for learning with noisy labels but do not explicitly model graph relations between samples.
16 Header: 2.2 Noise-robust learning on graphs
17 Para: Graph learning under label noise introduces an additional effect: message passing can propagate erroneous supervision through connected nodes. One line of work therefore uses graph structure as evidence for estimating label reliability. UnionNET, NRGNN, and RTGNN derive neighborhood evidence, augment reliable supervision, or coordinate dual networks [9,10,46]. Topology-guided selection and disagreement methods, including TSS, TFR, ProCon, GD², and GNN-CFGD, identify unreliable nodes from reconstruction, consistency, predictive discrepancy, or training history [40–42,44,47].
18 Para: A second line of work uses graph structure to generate or propagate more reliable supervision. DND-NET constructs robust pseudo-labels from neighborhood uncertainty, while RNCGLN combines graph contrastive representation learning with label self-correction under label and structure perturbations [11,12]. Prototype-Guided Supervision aggregates noisy labels into class-level prototypes, graph-teacher methods such as BO-NNC and SAMTD improve the source of supervision, and GTA, MILAB, and HALF adjust topology or aggregation to reduce the influence of unreliable neighborhoods [45,50,51,58,59,63]. NoisyGL [13] provides a unified benchmark for this family of methods.
19 Para: These methods establish graph structure as useful evidence for identifying unreliable labels and refining supervision. Their inference pipelines, however, generally continue to depend on the graph, leaving the graph-free deployment problem unresolved.
20 Header: 2.3 Graph-free node classification
21 Para: Graph-free methods avoid graph-dependent online prediction by exploiting graph structure during training. Graph-MLP incorporates adjacency through a neighborhood-contrastive objective, whereas GLNN distills predictions from a GNN teacher into an MLP; both deploy an attribute-only MLP [3,4]. Other methods transfer graph information through self-distillation, representation alignment, or precomputed structural signals [5,14,15,27,33]. They mainly differ in how graph information is transferred during training and whether graph-derived inputs are retained at inference.
22 Para: Teacher-based methods broaden the knowledge transferred to an MLP. FF-G2M separates low- and high-frequency components, KRD selects reliable teacher knowledge according to prediction stability, and VQGraph represents local structures with discrete codes [6,7,26]. PGKD and TINED transfer class prototypes or combine parameter injection with Dirichlet-energy distillation [28,43], while AdaGMLP, ProGMLP, and SALE-MLP study adaptive transfer, accuracy–cost trade-offs, and structure-aware latent representations [29–31]. Yu et al. [32] theoretically explain how GNN soft labels convey graph-induced low-frequency bias to an MLP.
23 Para: Graph-based label smoothing is closely related to GB-LCSS because it also modifies the training targets. PosteL estimates posterior soft labels from neighboring labels and graph-wide label co-occurrence statistics, ALS adjusts label-smoothing strength during graph training, and SALS incorporates neighborhood information into the target [60–62]. GB-LCSS also modifies the training targets, but defines its voting groups by geometry in a frozen, label-free graph representation rather than directly by graph adjacency. Each training node combines its observed label with the label consensus of the other training nodes in the same granular ball.
24 Header: 2.4 Graph contrastive learning and granular-ball learning
25 Para: Graph contrastive learning provides graph-aware representations without node labels by enforcing consistency between augmented graph views. GRACE constructs stochastic views through edge dropping and feature masking and aligns the representations of corresponding nodes across those views [16]. In GB-LCSS, this representation-learning stage is instantiated with GRACE and completed before labels are aggregated. The resulting geometry captures graph context while keeping regional partitioning independent of noisy labels.
26 Para: Granular-ball computing represents samples with coarse-grained hyperspherical regions for multi-granularity learning [34]. GBkNN classifies queries over granular balls, while granular-ball sampling uses ball purity and boundary structure to select representative samples [34,37]. Noise-oriented extensions employ granular regions for coarse representation and classification, reliable-sample selection, pseudo-label construction, or adaptive error correction [35,36,52,53]. In deep learning, GBRAIN, G-GBC, and BNO-GB use granular regions as coarse training units or as regions for distinguishing different forms of noisy samples [48,49,57]. Granular balls have also served as supernodes or hierarchical regions in supervised graph coarsening, GBGC, and MGGR [54–56].
27 Para: Prior studies mainly use granular balls as classifiers, sample-selection structures, coarse training units, pseudo-label regions, or graph-coarsening units. GB-LCSS instead uses them as a regional interface for supervision construction: the balls are formed without labels, their statistics are reused to build node-specific targets, and classification remains with an MLP operating on individual node attributes. Within this design, graph contrastive learning supplies the geometry and granular balls organize the local evidence.
28 Header: 3 Method
29 Header: 3.1 Problem setting and notation
30 Para: Consider a transductive attributed graph $G = (\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},X)$, where $\mathcal{V} = \{ v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}$ is the node set and $\mathcal{E}$ is the edge set. Let $A \in \{ 0,1\}^{N \times N}$ be the adjacency matrix and $X = \lbrack x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\rbrack^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ the node-attribute matrix. Each node belongs to one of $C$ classes. Let $\mathcal{V}_{L}$ denote the set of training nodes. The observed label ${\widetilde{y}}_{v} \in \{ 1,\ldots,C\}$ of a training node may be corrupted by an unknown noise process. GB-LCSS does not require prior knowledge of the noise type, noise rate, or label transition matrix.
31 Para: We consider transductive graph-free inference on a fixed graph. During training, the full node-attribute matrix $X$ and adjacency matrix $A$, including the test nodes, are available for representation learning and target construction, while test labels remain hidden. Only the classifier $f_{\theta}$ is retained after training. For a node $v$, its predictive distribution is
32 Para: $p_{\theta}( \cdot \mid x_{v}) = softmax\left( f_{\theta}(x_{v}) \right),$ (1)
33 Para: where $x_{v}$ is the classifier’s only input at inference; the adjacency matrix $A$, neighboring nodes, the frozen representation, and the graph encoder are unavailable to the classifier.
34 Para: Let $\mathcal{B} = \{ B_{j}\}_{j = 1}^{K}$ be a granular-ball partition covering all nodes, and let $T_{j} = B_{j} \cap \mathcal{V}_{L}$ be the training nodes in ball $B_{j}$. For $v \in T_{j}$, the subscript $- v$ indicates that the label of node $v$ is removed when local consensus is constructed. Other symbols are defined at first use.
35 Header: 3.2 Overview of GB-LCSS
36 Para: Figure 1 summarizes the four stages of GB-LCSS. First, the method learns a label-free node representation from attributes and graph connectivity, providing geometry for regional partitioning without using noisy labels. Second, recursive 2-means splitting partitions the frozen representation into a bounded set of disjoint granular balls. Third, GB-LCSS computes one label histogram per ball and subtracts each training node's own vote to construct its local consensus. Agreement among the remaining labels determines how much weight this consensus receives relative to the node's observed label. Finally, these node-specific targets train an MLP, and only this MLP is retained for graph-free inference.
37 FIGURE: Fig. 1. Overview of GB-LCSS. Stage 1 learns a label-free representation, and Stage 2 constructs granular balls in that representation space. Stage 3 enlarges a representative ball to show how a leave-one-out local-consensus target is formed. Stage 4 trains the deployed MLP with the resulting soft targets. Hollow nodes do not vote. Ball outlines are schematic, and each node belongs to exactly one ball. Graph structure and the learned representation are used only during training, whereas the classifier receives only node attributes at inference.
38 Header: 3.3 Label-agnostic granular-ball construction 
39 Header: 3.3.1 Graph contrastive representation
40 Para: To construct local regions that reflect node attributes and graph connectivity without using noisy labels, we instantiate the representation-learning component with GRACE [16]. The component forms two stochastic graph views through edge dropping and feature masking, then aligns the representations of the same node across views. Pretraining uses $X$ and $A$ but no node labels. After pretraining, we use the encoder output before the GRACE projection head as the representation
41 Para: $H = g_{\psi}(X,A) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_{h}},$ (2)
42 Para: which we store in node order after freezing $\psi$. Before constructing the balls, each node representation is L2-normalized with $\varepsilon = 10^{- 12}$ as a numerical safeguard:
43 Para: ${\bar{h}}_{v} = \frac{h_{v}}{max(\left\| h_{v} \right\|_{2},\varepsilon)}.$ (3)
44 Para: The normalized representation matrix is denoted by $\bar{H} = \lbrack{\bar{h}}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{h}}_{N}\rbrack^{\top}$. Throughout the main experiments, the frozen GRACE representation defines the label-free geometry used for granular-ball construction. The partition is determined entirely by this geometry. Training labels are introduced only afterward to construct local-consensus targets. Appendix D.3 examines alternative representation sources and variation across GRACE pretraining seeds.
45 Header: 3.3.2 Granular-ball construction
46 Para: The partitioning stage converts the frozen representation into a bounded set of compact, disjoint regions. Starting from one ball containing all nodes, GB-LCSS considers the most dispersed current regions first and accepts a split only when it sufficiently reduces within-region dispersion. The granular-ball set is initialized as $\mathcal{B} = \{\mathcal{V}\}$. For a ball $B \subseteq \mathcal{V}$, its center and mean distance to the center are defined as
47 Para: $\mu_{B} = \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{v \in B}^{}{\bar{h}}_{v},\quad\quad D(B) = \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{v \in B}^{}\left\| {\bar{h}}_{v} - \mu_{B} \right\|_{2}.$ (4)
48 Para: Each granular ball is represented by its member set and center, while $D(B)$ is its mean radial dispersion for evaluating candidate splits. A smaller $D(B)$ indicates a more compact region in the representation space. At each iteration, we collect candidate balls satisfying $|B| \geq 2n_{\min}$ and attempt 2-means splits in descending order of $D(B)$. The finite split budget is therefore allocated first to geometrically diffuse regions. If a candidate ball $B$ is partitioned into $B^{(1)}$ and $B^{(2)}$, the size-weighted dispersion after splitting is
49 Para: $D_{split}(B) = \frac{|B^{(1)}|D(B^{(1)}) + |B^{(2)}|D(B^{(2)})}{|B|}.$ (5)
50 Para: In each iteration, we accept the first candidate split for which both child balls contain at least $n_{\min}$ nodes and
51 Para: $D_{split}(B) \leq (1 - \delta)D(B).$ (6)
52 Para: Here, $n_{\min}$ is a positive integer and $0 < \delta < 1$. Equation (6) requires the weighted dispersion to decrease by at least a fraction $\delta$, while the child-size constraint prevents very small regions. After each accepted split, the candidates and their ordering are recomputed. Recursion stops when an iteration contains no valid split or the number of balls reaches the prescribed ball budget $B_{\max}$, where $B_{\max}$ is a positive integer. The final partition contains at most $B_{\max}$ disjoint granular balls, covers all nodes, and assigns each node to exactly one ball. Section 4.4 gives the experimental parameters.
53 Header: 3.4 Granular-ball local-consensus soft supervision
54 Header: 3.4.1 Leave-one-out consensus target
55 Para: After the label-free partition has been fixed, GB-LCSS uses the observed labels of the training nodes within each ball to construct local supervision. One label histogram is computed per ball and reused across its training nodes. For each training node $v$, the node-specific leave-one-out counts are obtained by subtracting $v$'s own vote.
56 Para: For a granular ball $B_{j}$, let $T_{j} = B_{j} \cap \mathcal{V}_{L}$ denote its training nodes. To prevent a node’s observed label from contributing to the local evidence used to adjust that same label, GB-LCSS excludes the node from the within-ball vote and defines the voting set for each $v \in T_{j}$ as
57 Para: $T_{j, - v} = T_{j}\backslash\{ v\}.$ (7)
58 Para: When $|T_{j, - v}| \geq s_{\min}$, the noisy labels of the other training nodes in the ball define the empirical label distribution
59 Para: $p_{j, - v,c} = \frac{1}{|T_{j, - v}|}\sum_{u \in T_{j, - v}}^{}\mathbb{I}({\widetilde{y}}_{u} = c),\quad\quad c = 1,\ldots,C.$ (8)
60 Para: Let
61 Para: $M_{j, - v} = \{ c:p_{j, - v,c} = r_{j, - v}\},\quad\quad r_{j, - v} = \max_{c}p_{j, - v,c}.$ (9)
62 Para: $M_{j, - v}$ is the set of classes tied for the largest number of votes after node $v$ has been removed. Ties are determined directly from integer vote counts. If the most-voted class is unique, denote it by $m_{j, - v}$ and mix its one-hot vector with the empirical distribution. If multiple classes are tied, retain the empirical distribution:
63 Para: $q_{j, - v} = \left\{ \begin{matrix}
(1 - \beta)p_{j, - v} + \beta e_{m_{j, - v}}, & |M_{j, - v}| = 1, \\
p_{j, - v}, & |M_{j, - v}| > 1.
\end{matrix} \right.\ $ (10)
64 Para: Here, $e_{c}$ is the one-hot vector for class $c$, and $\beta \in \lbrack 0,1\rbrack$ controls the mixture of the empirical distribution and the one-hot vector of the most-voted class. Under a tie, no class is selected by its index. Because a different node is removed each time, nodes in the same granular ball may have different $p_{j, - v}$ and $q_{j, - v}$.
65 Header: 3.4.2 Normalized consensus weight and node target
66 Para: The weight controls how strongly local consensus changes the observed label. We assign more weight to the consensus when a larger proportion of the remaining labels agree on one class. We therefore normalize the largest vote proportion against the uniform class probability $1/C$:
67 Para: $\alpha_{j, - v} = max\left( 0,\frac{r_{j, - v} - 1/C}{1 - 1/C} \right).$ (11)
68 Para: $\alpha_{j, - v}$ measures the concentration of the leave-one-out label distribution relative to a uniform distribution, whereas the positive integer $s_{\min}$ specifies the minimum number of leave-one-out votes required to use local consensus. The two quantities therefore describe distributional concentration and effective vote count, respectively. For $v \in T_{j}$, the final training target is
69 Para: $t_{v} = \left\{ \begin{matrix}
(1 - \alpha_{j, - v})e_{{\widetilde{y}}_{v}} + \alpha_{j, - v}q_{j, - v}, & |T_{j, - v}| \geq s_{\min}, \\
e_{{\widetilde{y}}_{v}}, & |T_{j, - v}| < s_{\min}.
\end{matrix} \right.\ $ (12)
70 Para: When the leave-one-out vote is close to uniform, $\alpha_{j, - v}$ is small and the target remains close to the node’s observed label. As the label distribution becomes more concentrated, the supervision shifts toward the local consensus $q_{j, - v}$. The second branch retains the observed label when too few votes remain. If at least $s_{\min}$ votes remain and all agree, the target becomes the one-hot label of their shared class.
71 Para: Equations (7)–(12) use only the observed labels of training nodes; validation and test labels do not enter target construction or the training loss.

73 Header: 3.4.3 Graph-free MLP training
74 Para: The student $f_{\theta}$ is a two-layer MLP. Its hidden linear layer is followed by ReLU, batch normalization, and dropout, and its output layer produces the class logits. Its predictive distribution is given by Eq. (1), and it minimizes the soft cross-entropy over the training nodes:
75 Para: $\mathcal{L}_{LCSS} = - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{L}|}\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}_{L}}^{}{\sum_{c = 1}^{C}t_{vc}}\log p_{\theta}(c \mid x_{v}).$ (13)

80 Header: 3.5 Consensus-teacher distillation extension
81 Para: Basic GB-LCSS provides direct consensus targets only for training nodes. To extend this supervision across the complete graph, the distillation variant trains a graph teacher on the same targets and then trains the MLP student to match the teacher's node-wise predictions for all nodes. The teacher therefore serves as a graph-based intermediary during training, while the deployed model remains the same MLP.
82 Para: The teacher is trained directly with the GB-LCSS targets defined in Section 3.4. For each training node $v \in T_{j}$, target construction follows the same leave-one-out vote and low-support fallback as basic GB-LCSS. The teacher target is
83 Para: $t_{v}^{T} = t_{v},v \in \mathcal{V}_{L}.$ (14)
84 Para: Here, $t_{v}$ is defined in Eq. (12). In the extension, this target supervises a graph-structured teacher. The teacher $g_{\phi}$ is a two-layer GCN that takes the original node attributes $X$ and adjacency matrix $A$ as input:
85 Para: $Z^{T} = g_{\phi}(X,A).$ (15)
86 Para: The frozen representation $\bar{H}$ determines the granular balls and their consensus targets but is not used as a teacher input. The teacher minimizes the following objective over training nodes:
87 Para: $\mathcal{L}_{T} = - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{L}|}\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}_{L}}^{}{\sum_{c = 1}^{C}t_{vc}^{T}}logsoftmax\left( Z_{v}^{T} \right)_{c}.$ (16)
88 Para: After selecting a teacher checkpoint, we freeze $g_{\phi}$ and compute temperature-scaled soft predictions for all nodes, where $\tau > 0$ is the distillation temperature:
89 Para: $\pi_{v} = softmax\left( \frac{Z_{v}^{T}}{\tau} \right).$ (17)
90 Para: The MLP student then fits these predictions on all $N$ nodes:
91 Para: $\mathcal{L}_{KD} = - \frac{\tau^{2}}{N}\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}}^{}{\sum_{c = 1}^{C}\pi_{vc}}logsoftmax\left( \frac{f_{\theta}(x_{v})}{\tau} \right)_{c}.$ (18)
92 Para: The basic method applies local-consensus targets only to $\mathcal{V}_{L}$, whereas Eq. (18) fits teacher predictions on every node. The student loss consists solely of cross-entropy against the teacher’s soft predictions. After training, only the student MLP is retained; the teacher, adjacency matrix, and frozen representation are not used for inference.
93 Header: 3.6 Computational complexity and inference resources
94 Para: Before training the MLP, basic GB-LCSS performs graph contrastive pretraining followed by granular-ball construction and soft-target generation. The frozen representation requires $O(Nd_{h})$ storage and can be reused across noise settings and downstream seeds on the same dataset. Because the cost of GRACE depends on the encoder configuration and pretraining schedule, Section 5.5 reports its measured runtime and peak GPU memory; the analysis below focuses on post-pretraining ball construction and target generation.
95 Para: On the frozen representation, running 2-means for $I$ iterations on a node subset $S$ costs $O(I|S|d_{h})$. Candidate balls processed within the same iteration are disjoint, so their total number of nodes is at most $N$. Because at most $B_{\max} - 1$ splits can be accepted, a conservative upper bound for granular-ball construction is
96 Para: $O(IB_{\max}Nd_{h}).$ (19)
97 Para: For fixed $I$ and $B_{\max}$, Eq. (19) is linear in the number of nodes $N$ and the representation dimension $d_{h}$. During construction, the frozen representation requires $O(Nd_{h})$ space, while node assignments, ball centers, and class histograms add $O(N + B_{\max}(d_{h} + C))$. Once the ball-level histograms are available, all training targets can be generated in $O(|\mathcal{V}_{L}|C)$ time by subtracting each node's vote from its ball histogram. The current implementation stores the targets in an $N \times C$ matrix, adding $O(NC)$ space.
98 Para: After target construction, student optimization is the same as for the standard two-layer MLP. Let $F$ denote the hidden width. With full-batch training, each epoch evaluates the MLP for all $N$ nodes and applies the loss on the training-node mask; over $T_{S}$ epochs, this costs $O(T_{S}N(dF + FC))$. The per-node inference cost is
99 Para: $O(dF + FC),$ (20)
100 Para: At deployment, only the student parameters, of order $O(dF + FC)$, are retained. The adjacency matrix, frozen GRACE representation, and granular-ball partition are not required, so the per-node inference cost in Eq. (20) is independent of the number of edges $|\mathcal{E}|$. The distillation extension adds the training cost of a two-layer GCN teacher, which is also removed after training. Section 5.5 further reports measured GRACE pretraining time and memory together with grouping-construction time and storage.

106 Header: 4.2 Data splits and label noise
107 Para: We used a transductive node-classification protocol on a fixed graph. The node attributes and adjacency of the complete graph were available during training, so the attributes and topology of test nodes provided unlabeled context for GRACE pretraining and granular-ball partitioning. For seeds 0–4, nodes were randomly sampled without class stratification using the NoisyGL percentage-split routine, with target sizes $\lfloor 0.2N\rfloor$, $\lfloor 0.1N\rfloor$, and $\lfloor 0.7N\rfloor$ for training, validation, and testing, respectively. All methods shared the same split for a given dataset and seed. Under the default protocol, noise was injected only into training labels. Clean validation labels were used for checkpoint selection, and test labels were used only for final evaluation. Test-node classification accuracy was the primary evaluation metric. Appendix C additionally examines checkpoint selection when validation labels are corrupted.
108 Para: Uniform noise satisfies $P_{ii} = 1 - \rho$ and $P_{ij} = \rho/(C - 1)$, with $\rho \in \{ 0.2,0.4,0.6\}$. Pair noise uses $\rho = 0.4$ and flips class $i$ to $((i - 2)\ mod\ C) + 1$ with probability $\rho$, leaving it unchanged with probability $1 - \rho$. Instance-dependent noise was generated with the NoisyGL routine at a configured mean flip rate of 0.4 and $\text{norm\_std} = 0.1$. For each dataset and seed, all methods shared the same noisy training-label realization.
109 Header: 4.3 Compared methods and comparison groups
110 Para: The first group consists of noisy-label methods implemented under a common two-layer MLP backbone: MLP+CE, Small-loss, Co-teaching, JoCoR, SCE, ELR, LogitClip, and GB-LCSS. All methods use the same labeled training nodes and receive only node attributes at inference. Small-loss ranks samples by per-sample cross-entropy and retains the small-loss subset according to the forget-rate schedule. GB-LCSS uses training-time graph information to construct supervision under our problem setting. Co-teaching and JoCoR retain two independently initialized two-layer MLPs during training; evaluation uses one network without ensembling their predictions.
111 Para: The second group comprises structure-assisted methods evaluated under the graph-free inference protocol of this study: PosteL, ALS, SALS [60–62], Graph-MLP [4], and GSDN [14]. These methods may use graph information during training but use only node attributes at inference. PosteL integrates its official posterior-target construction path with iterative pseudo-labeling disabled; the remaining methods retain their published core objectives or mechanisms under our data splits, noise realizations, and common MLP student. Appendix G reports the implementation provenance, protocol alignment, and method-specific parameters.
112 Para: The graph-teacher extension compares graph-to-MLP knowledge-transfer routes under a common teacher–student protocol. The basic response-distillation baseline follows the GNN-to-MLP framework of GLNN [3]. We additionally compare FF-G2M, KRD, VQGraph, and the granular-ball consensus teacher [6,7,26]. FF-G2M is reimplemented with common teacher and student backbones while retaining its low- and high-frequency distillation objectives; KRD and VQGraph retain their reliable-knowledge selection and soft-codebook mechanisms. Both teacher and student are retrained for every seed, and the final student uses only node attributes.
113 Header: 4.4 Implementation details
114 Para: All datasets were loaded through PyTorch Geometric using the NoisyGL data interface. GRACE pretraining used row-normalized node attributes. Downstream models used the node attributes supplied by NoisyGL. Graph-convolutional models additionally used its adjacency matrix, with normalization and self-loops handled within the graph-convolution layers.
115 Para: All basic methods used the same two-layer MLP backbone with a hidden width of 64 and a dropout rate of 0.5. Dual-network methods used two independently initialized copies of this backbone. Models in this comparison group were trained for 200 epochs with Adam, a learning rate of 0.01, and weight decay of $5 \times 10^{- 4}$. The checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy was retained, and no patience-based early stopping was used. The same validation-accuracy criterion was used for teacher checkpoint selection. Small-loss, Co-teaching, and JoCoR set their forget-rate schedules from the known synthetic noise rate; the other basic methods did not use this information.
116 Para: GB-LCSS used $B_{\max} = 64$, $n_{\min} = 32$, $\delta = 0.05$, $s_{\min} = 2$, and $\beta = 0.5$ in all experiments. The 2-means procedure used Euclidean distance and k-means++ initialization. The downstream seed also controlled the 2-means initialization, and each candidate split used 10 initializations. GRACE used label-free, dataset-level configurations, and its encoder was frozen after pretraining. The distillation temperature was $\tau = 1$. Appendix G gives the complete parameters and their sources.
117 Header: 4.5 Statistical analysis
118 Para: We report the mean test accuracy and sample standard deviation over five paired seeds. The main experiments used one pretrained representation $H$ for each dataset. The five paired runs varied the data split, noise realization, downstream initialization, and, for GB-based methods, the 2-means initialization. Appendix D.3 provides a diagnostic across GRACE pretraining seeds. For each dataset and noise setting, we used a two-sided paired $t$-test, implemented as a one-sample $t$-test on the five same-seed accuracy differences, and computed 95% confidence intervals from a $t$ distribution with four degrees of freedom. Holm correction was applied separately to three families of 25 comparisons: GB-LCSS versus random size-matched grouping, MLP+CE, and JoCoR. These families represent the grouping mechanism control, the standard MLP baseline, and a strong noisy-label baseline, respectively. These tests quantify the stability of paired differences across the five downstream seeds. Appendix B reports the complete paired results.

141 Header: 5.3.2 Role of the granular-ball partition
142 Para: The kNN and k-means alternatives use the same leave-one-out target as the main method. For each seed, kNN matches its neighborhood size to the mean number of training votes per ball and excludes the query node. The number of k-means clusters matches the number of granular balls for that seed, and the current node is likewise removed from its cluster target. Relative to the better of the two alternatives, granular balls differ by $+ 0.27$, $- 0.94$, $- 3.11$, $- 0.29$, and $- 0.11$ percentage points on the five full-spectrum datasets. Except on CoraFull, the gap among the three grouping implementations remains below one percentage point. Local-consensus supervision therefore does not depend on a unique grouping rule.
143 Para: We retain granular balls as the default regional interface in GB-LCSS because the budgeted recursive partition automatically determines a bounded set of disjoint regions and supports shared regional statistics. In the matched k-means control, by contrast, the cluster count is supplied by the number of granular balls produced for the same seed. kNN maintains overlapping and potentially asymmetric neighborhoods for individual training nodes and aggregates their labels separately.

Except on CiteSeer, GB-LCSS does not improve accuracy with clean labels.
146 Para: This pattern indicates that the observed advantage of GB-LCSS over MLP+CE is concentrated in the noisy-label settings evaluated here.

151 Header: 5.6 Discussion
152 Para: Across the full-spectrum and supplementary evaluations, geometry-derived grouping is more effective than random size-matched grouping. Gains over strong noisy-label classifiers and alternative grouping interfaces vary across datasets, and clean-label training shows no consistent additional benefit. These results support local-consensus soft-target construction for noisy-label learning, while showing that the choice of grouping interface remains dataset dependent.

166 Header: 7 Conclusion
167 Para: This study addresses transductive node classification with noisy training labels when graph structure is available during training but inference is limited to node attributes. GB-LCSS forms disjoint, label-agnostic regions in a graph contrastive representation and converts leave-one-out local consensus into node-specific targets for the deployed MLP. Across five datasets and five noise settings, it achieves the highest or tied-highest five-seed mean in 24 of 25 settings among seven noise-robust baselines using the same deployed MLP architecture. Averaged over these settings, GB-LCSS also has higher mean accuracy than PosteL, ALS, and SALS under the common protocol.
168 Para: Granular balls organize supervision into a bounded set of disjoint regions. Within each ball, a single label histogram is reused by subtracting each training node's own vote. Alternative neighborhood and clustering rules also support local consensus. As a secondary extension, a graph teacher trained on the same targets supplies predictions over all nodes and improves the MLP student's mean accuracy under the evaluated protocol without changing the deployment interface.
169 Para: The experiments use synthetic label noise, clean validation labels, and transductive evaluation on fixed graphs; target construction also depends on a pretrained and frozen graph representation. Future work should examine real annotation noise, model selection without clean validation labels, inductive inference for new nodes, and robustness across representation-pretraining runs.
