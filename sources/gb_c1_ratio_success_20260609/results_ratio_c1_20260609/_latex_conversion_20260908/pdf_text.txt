Granular-Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision for Noisy-Label Transductive Node
Classiﬁcation with Graph-Free Inference
Abstract
In many deployment scenarios, graph structure is available during training, whereas inference must rely only on node
attributes because relations are missing or online graph processing is constrained. Methods for learning with noisy labels
on graphs generally retain graph-dependent inference, while graph-free node classiﬁcation has largely been studied with
clean supervision. We propose Granular-Ball Local-Consensus Soft Supervision (GB-LCSS) for transductive node
classiﬁcation with noisy labels and graph-free inference. GB-LCSS ﬁrst learns label-free node representations from
attributes and graph connectivity and recursively partitions the resulting space into a bounded set of disjoint granular
balls. For each training node, GB-LCSS aggregates the labels of the other training nodes in the same ball and adaptively
blends their consensus with the node’s observed label to form a soft target. Label-free region construction prevents
noisy annotations from shaping the partition, while the adaptive target reduces the inﬂuence of isolated label errors
when local evidence is consistent. Across ﬁve datasets and ﬁve noise settings, GB-LCSS achieves the highest or tied-
highest ﬁve-seed mean accuracy in 24 of 25 settings among seven noisy-label baselines using the same deployed MLP
architecture, while requiring only node attributes at inference.
Keywords: label noise, node classiﬁcation, graph-free inference, graph contrastive learning, granular-ball computing,
knowledge distillation
1. Introduction
Graph neural networks (GNNs) are eﬀective for node classiﬁcation because message passing combines node attributes
with relational context [ 1, 2]. This dependency also complicates deployment: prediction requires access to the graph and
repeated neighborhood aggregation, which may be costly or infeasible when relations are unavailable or online graph
processing is restricted. Graph-free approaches such as Graph-MLP and GLNN exploit graph structure during training
but deploy an MLP that predicts directly from node attributes [ 3, 4]. This removes graph access and neighborhood
aggregation from online prediction.
Noisy training labels introduce a second challenge. Labels collected through manual annotation, automated rules,
or multiple sources can contain errors, and deep models may eventually memorize corrupted supervision [ 8]. On graphs,
message passing can further spread the eﬀect of mislabeled nodes [ 13]. Noise-robust graph methods use topology for
label correction, pseudo-labeling, sample selection, or robust representation learning [ 9–12, 47], but their predictions
generally remain graph dependent. Existing graph-free studies, by contrast, have largely focused on structural knowledge
transfer with clean supervision. Robustness to noisy training labels remains insuﬃciently studied in graph-free node
classiﬁcation.
To improve supervision without introducing graph dependence at inference, GB-LCSS uses the training graph to
construct soft targets for the deployed MLP. The method ﬁrst learns label-free node representations with a graph
contrastive encoder instantiated by GRACE [ 16], so label noise does not alter the geometry used for partitioning. It
then recursively divides this representation space into a bounded set of disjoint granular balls [ 34]. For each training
node, GB-LCSS counts the observed labels of the other training nodes in the same ball to form a local consensus.
Greater agreement among these labels gives the consensus more weight, helping counter isolated label errors, whereas

We evaluate GB-LCSS on Cora, CiteSeer, CoraFull, Coauthor-CS, and Amazon-Photo under uniform, pair, and

The main contributions are as follows:
1. We study noisy-label transductive node classiﬁcation with graph-free inference, using training-time graph structure
to improve supervision while the deployed classiﬁer uses only node attributes at inference.
2. We introduce GB-LCSS, which partitions a label-free graph contrastive representation into disjoint granular balls
and uses label agreement among the other training nodes in each ball to reﬁne each node’s observed label into a soft
target.
3. We evaluate GB-LCSS against noise-robust and structure-assisted graph-free methods across ﬁve datasets and ﬁve
noise settings. Controlled grouping and target analyses identify the contribution of local consensus, and a secondary
graph-teacher extension examines whether node-wise predictions over all nodes can further improve the MLP student.
2. Related work
2.1. Learning with noisy labels
Learning with noisy labels reduces the inﬂuence of corrupted annotations by controlling which samples are trusted,
how supervision is represented, or how strongly a model ﬁts uncertain targets. Sample-selection methods exploit the
tendency of deep networks to ﬁt clean patterns before memorizing noise. Co-teaching exchanges small-loss samples
between two networks, while JoCoR also enforces agreement between their predictions [ 17, 18]. DivideMix treats the
separated clean and noisy samples as a semi-supervised learning problem, whereas FINE and CT estimate sample
reliability from class-wise feature directions and conﬁdence trajectories [ 23–25].
A complementary family modiﬁes the training objective. SCE combines cross-entropy with reverse cross-entropy,
ELR suppresses late-stage memorization, and ANL and LogitClip reduce sensitivity to corrupted targets through
negative learning and logit-norm control [ 19–22]. These methods provide general strategies for learning with noisy
labels but do not explicitly model graph relations between samples.
2.2. Noise-robust learning on graphs
Graph learning under label noise introduces an additional eﬀect: message passing can propagate erroneous su-
pervision through connected nodes. One line of work therefore uses graph structure as evidence for estimating label
reliability. UnionNET, NRGNN, and RTGNN derive neighborhood evidence, augment reliable supervision, or coordi-
nate dual networks [ 9, 10, 46]. Topology-guided selection and disagreement methods, including TSS, TFR, ProCon,
GD2, and GNN-CFGD, identify unreliable nodes from reconstruction, consistency, predictive discrepancy, or training
history [ 40–42, 44, 47].
A second line of work uses graph structure to generate or propagate more reliable supervision. DND-NET constructs
robust pseudo-labels from neighborhood uncertainty, while RNCGLN combines graph contrastive representation learn-
ing with label self-correction under label and structure perturbations [ 11, 12]. Prototype-Guided Supervision aggregates
noisy labels into class-level prototypes, graph-teacher methods such as BO-NNC and SAMTD improve the source of
supervision, and GTA, MILAB, and HALF adjust topology or aggregation to reduce the inﬂuence of unreliable neigh-
borhoods [ 45, 50, 51, 58, 59, 63]. NoisyGL [ 13] provides a uniﬁed benchmark for this family of methods.

These methods establish graph structure as useful evidence for identifying unreliable labels and reﬁning supervision.
Their inference pipelines, however, generally continue to depend on the graph, leaving the graph-free deployment
problem unresolved.
2.3. Graph-free node classiﬁcation
Graph-free methods avoid graph-dependent online prediction by exploiting graph structure during training. Graph-
MLP incorporates adjacency through a neighborhood-contrastive objective, whereas GLNN distills predictions from
a GNN teacher into an MLP; both deploy an attribute-only MLP [ 3, 4]. Other methods transfer graph information
through self-distillation, representation alignment, or precomputed structural signals [ 5, 14, 15, 27, 33]. They mainly
diﬀer in how graph information is transferred during training and whether graph-derived inputs are retained at inference.
Teacher-based methods broaden the knowledge transferred to an MLP. FF-G2M separates low- and high-frequency
components, KRD selects reliable teacher knowledge according to prediction stability, and VQGraph represents local
structures with discrete codes [ 6, 7, 26]. PGKD and TINED transfer class prototypes or combine parameter injec-
tion with Dirichlet-energy distillation [ 28, 43], while AdaGMLP, ProGMLP, and SALE-MLP study adaptive transfer,
accuracy–cost trade-oﬀs, and structure-aware latent representations [ 29–31]. Yu et al. [ 32] theoretically explain how
GNN soft labels convey graph-induced low-frequency bias to an MLP.
Graph-based label smoothing is closely related to GB-LCSS because it also modiﬁes the training targets. PosteL
estimates posterior soft labels from neighboring labels and graph-wide label co-occurrence statistics, ALS adjusts label-
smoothing strength during graph training, and SALS incorporates neighborhood information into the target [ 60–62].
GB-LCSS also modiﬁes the training targets, but deﬁnes its voting groups by geometry in a frozen, label-free graph
representation rather than directly by graph adjacency. Each training node combines its observed label with the label
consensus of the other training nodes in the same granular ball.
2.4. Graph contrastive learning and granular-ball learning
Graph contrastive learning provides graph-aware representations without node labels by enforcing consistency be-
tween augmented graph views. GRACE constructs stochastic views through edge dropping and feature masking and
aligns the representations of corresponding nodes across those views [ 16]. In GB-LCSS, this representation-learning
stage is instantiated with GRACE and completed before labels are aggregated. The resulting geometry captures graph
context while keeping regional partitioning independent of noisy labels.
Granular-ball computing represents samples with coarse-grained hyperspherical regions for multi-granularity learning
[34]. GBkNN classiﬁes queries over granular balls, while granular-ball sampling uses ball purity and boundary structure
to select representative samples [ 34, 37]. Noise-oriented extensions employ granular regions for coarse representation and
classiﬁcation, reliable-sample selection, pseudo-label construction, or adaptive error correction [ 35, 36, 52, 53]. In deep
learning, GBRAIN, G-GBC, and BNO-GB use granular regions as coarse training units or as regions for distinguishing
diﬀerent forms of noisy samples [ 48, 49, 57]. Granular balls have also served as supernodes or hierarchical regions in
supervised graph coarsening, GBGC, and MGGR [ 54–56].
Prior studies mainly use granular balls as classiﬁers, sample-selection structures, coarse training units, pseudo-label
regions, or graph-coarsening units. GB-LCSS instead uses them as a regional interface for supervision construction: the
balls are formed without labels, their statistics are reused to build node-speciﬁc targets, and classiﬁcation remains with
an MLP operating on individual node attributes. Within this design, graph contrastive learning supplies the geometry
and granular balls organize the local evidence.

3.2. Overview of GB-LCSS
Figure 1 summarizes the four stages of GB-LCSS. First, the method learns a label-free node representation from
attributes and graph connectivity, providing geometry for regional partitioning without using noisy labels. Second,
recursive 2-means splitting partitions the frozen representation into a bounded set of disjoint granular balls. Third,
GB-LCSS computes one label histogram per ball and subtracts each training node’s own vote to construct its local
consensus. Agreement among the remaining labels determines how much weight this consensus receives relative to the
node’s observed label. Finally, these node-speciﬁc targets train an MLP, and only this MLP is retained for graph-free
inference.

3.6. Computational complexity and inference resources
Before training the MLP, basic GB-LCSS performs graph contrastive pretraining followed by granular-ball construc-
tion and soft-target generation. The frozen representation requires O(N dh) storage and can be reused across noise
settings and downstream seeds on the same dataset. Because the cost of GRACE depends on the encoder conﬁguration
and pretraining schedule, Section 5.5 reports its measured runtime and peak GPU memory; the analysis below focuses
on post-pretraining ball construction and target generation.
On the frozen representation, running 2-means for I iterations on a node subset S costs O(I|S|dh). Candidate balls
processed within the same iteration are disjoint, so their total number of nodes is at most N . Because at most Bmax − 1
splits can be accepted, a conservative upper bound for granular-ball construction is
O(IB maxN dh). (19)
For ﬁxed I and Bmax, Eq. (19) is linear in the number of nodes N and the representation dimension dh. During
construction, the frozen representation requires O(N dh) space, while node assignments, ball centers, and class his-
tograms add O(N + Bmax(dh + C)). Once the ball-level histograms are available, all training targets can be generated
in O(|VL|C) time by subtracting each node’s vote from its ball histogram. The current implementation stores the
targets in an N × C matrix, adding O(N C) space.
After target construction, student optimization is the same as for the standard two-layer MLP. Let F denote the
hidden width. With full-batch training, each epoch evaluates the MLP for all N nodes and applies the loss on the
training-node mask; over TS epochs, this costs O(TSN (dF + F C)). The per-node inference cost is
O(dF + F C), (20)
At deployment, only the student parameters, of order O(dF + F C), are retained. The adjacency matrix, frozen
GRACE representation, and granular-ball partition are not required, so the per-node inference cost in Eq. (20) is
independent of the number of edges |E|. The distillation extension adds the training cost of a two-layer GCN teacher,
which is also removed after training. Section 5.5 further reports measured GRACE pretraining time and memory
together with grouping-construction time and storage.

4.2. Data splits and label noise
We used a transductive node-classiﬁcation protocol on a ﬁxed graph. The node attributes and adjacency of the
complete graph were available during training, so the attributes and topology of test nodes provided unlabeled context
for GRACE pretraining and granular-ball partitioning. For seeds 0–4, nodes were randomly sampled without class
stratiﬁcation using the NoisyGL percentage-split routine, with target sizes ⌊0.2N ⌋, ⌊0.1N ⌋, and ⌊0.7N ⌋ for training,
validation, and testing, respectively. All methods shared the same split for a given dataset and seed. Under the default
protocol, noise was injected only into training labels. Clean validation labels were used for checkpoint selection, and
test labels were used only for ﬁnal evaluation. Test-node classiﬁcation accuracy was the primary evaluation metric.
Appendix C additionally examines checkpoint selection when validation labels are corrupted.
Uniform noise satisﬁes Pii = 1 − ρ and Pij = ρ/(C − 1), with ρ ∈ { 0.2, 0.4, 0.6}. Pair noise uses ρ = 0 .4 and ﬂips
class i to (( i − 2) mod C) + 1 with probability ρ, leaving it unchanged with probability 1 − ρ. Instance-dependent noise
was generated with the NoisyGL routine at a conﬁgured mean ﬂip rate of 0.4 and norm std = 0 .1. For each dataset
and seed, all methods shared the same noisy training-label realization.
4.3. Compared methods and comparison groups
The ﬁrst group consists of noisy-label methods implemented under a common two-layer MLP backbone: MLP+CE,
Small-loss, Co-teaching, JoCoR, SCE, ELR, LogitClip, and GB-LCSS. All methods use the same labeled training nodes
and receive only node attributes at inference. Small-loss ranks samples by per-sample cross-entropy and retains the
small-loss subset according to the forget-rate schedule. GB-LCSS uses training-time graph information to construct
supervision under our problem setting. Co-teaching and JoCoR retain two independently initialized two-layer MLPs
during training; evaluation uses one network without ensembling their predictions.
The second group comprises structure-assisted methods evaluated under the graph-free inference protocol of this

The graph-teacher extension compares graph-to-MLP knowledge-transfer routes under a common teacher–student

with common teacher and student backbones while retaining its low- and high-frequency distillation objectives; KRD
and VQGraph retain their reliable-knowledge selection and soft-codebook mechanisms. Both teacher and student are
retrained for every seed, and the ﬁnal student uses only node attributes.
4.4. Implementation details
All datasets were loaded through PyTorch Geometric using the NoisyGL data interface. GRACE pretraining
used row-normalized node attributes. Downstream models used the node attributes supplied by NoisyGL. Graph-
convolutional models additionally used its adjacency matrix, with normalization and self-loops handled within the
graph-convolution layers.
All basic methods used the same two-layer MLP backbone with a hidden width of 64 and a dropout rate of 0.5.
Dual-network methods used two independently initialized copies of this backbone. Models in this comparison group
were trained for 200 epochs with Adam, a learning rate of 0.01, and weight decay of 5 × 10−4. The checkpoint with the
highest validation accuracy was retained, and no patience-based early stopping was used. The same validation-accuracy
criterion was used for teacher checkpoint selection. Small-loss, Co-teaching, and JoCoR set their forget-rate schedules
from the known synthetic noise rate; the other basic methods did not use this information.
GB-LCSS used Bmax = 64, nmin = 32, δ = 0.05, smin = 2, and β = 0.5 in all experiments. The 2-means procedure
used Euclidean distance and k-means++ initialization. The downstream seed also controlled the 2-means initialization,
and each candidate split used 10 initializations. GRACE used label-free, dataset-level conﬁgurations, and its encoder
was frozen after pretraining. The distillation temperature was τ = 1. Appendix G gives the complete parameters and
their sources.
4.5. Statistical analysis
We report the mean test accuracy and sample standard deviation over ﬁve paired seeds. The main experiments
used one pretrained representation H for each dataset. The ﬁve paired runs varied the data split, noise realization,
downstream initialization, and, for GB-based methods, the 2-means initialization. Appendix D.3 provides a diagnostic
across GRACE pretraining seeds. For each dataset and noise setting, we used a two-sided paired t-test, implemented as a
one-sample t-test on the ﬁve same-seed accuracy diﬀerences, and computed 95% conﬁdence intervals from a t distribution
with four degrees of freedom. Holm correction was applied separately to three families of 25 comparisons: GB-LCSS
versus random size-matched grouping, MLP+CE, and JoCoR. These families represent the grouping mechanism control,
the standard MLP baseline, and a strong noisy-label baseline, respectively. These tests quantify the stability of paired
diﬀerences across the ﬁve downstream seeds. Appendix B reports the complete paired results.

5.3.2. Role of the granular-ball partition
The kNN and k-means alternatives use the same leave-one-out target as the main method. For each seed, kNN
matches its neighborhood size to the mean number of training votes per ball and excludes the query node. The number
of k-means clusters matches the number of granular balls for that seed, and the current node is likewise removed from
its cluster target. Relative to the better of the two alternatives, granular balls diﬀer by +0 .27, −0.94, −3.11, −0.29,
and −0.11 percentage points on the ﬁve full-spectrum datasets. Except on CoraFull, the gap among the three grouping
implementations remains below one percentage point. Local-consensus supervision therefore does not depend on a
unique grouping rule.
We retain granular balls as the default regional interface in GB-LCSS because the budgeted recursive partition
automatically determines a bounded set of disjoint regions and supports shared regional statistics. In the matched
k-means control, by contrast, the cluster count is supplied by the number of granular balls produced for the same seed.
kNN maintains overlapping and potentially asymmetric neighborhoods for individual training nodes and aggregates
their labels separately.
5.4. Clean-label control
We also test whether GB-LCSS improves classiﬁcation with clean training labels by setting ρ=0 and keeping the
data splits and other training settings unchanged. The accuracy diﬀerences between GB-LCSS and MLP+CE on Cora,
CiteSeer, CoraFull, Coauthor-CS, and Amazon-Photo are −0.24, +2 .00, −0.38, −0.87, and −1.75 percentage points,
respectively. Except on CiteSeer, GB-LCSS does not improve accuracy with clean labels.
This pattern indicates that the observed advantage of GB-LCSS over MLP+CE is concentrated in the noisy-label
settings evaluated here.

5.6. Discussion
Across the full-spectrum and supplementary evaluations, geometry-derived grouping is more eﬀective than random
size-matched grouping. Gains over strong noisy-label classiﬁers and alternative grouping interfaces vary across datasets,
and clean-label training shows no consistent additional beneﬁt. These results support local-consensus soft-target con-
struction for noisy-label learning, while showing that the choice of grouping interface remains dataset dependent.
6. Graph-teacher distillation extension
6.1. From training-node targets to distillation over all nodes
Basic GB-LCSS directly supervises only the training nodes. The distillation extension broadens this coverage by
training a GCN teacher on the same targets and using its predictions over all nodes to supervise the MLP student. The
teacher is used only during training, so the deployed model remains the same attribute-only MLP.
We evaluated the extension on Cora, CiteSeer, CoraFull, Coauthor-CS, and Amazon-Photo under uniform 0.4 and
0.6 to compare diﬀerent forms of teacher supervision and knowledge transfer.

7. Conclusion
This study addresses transductive node classiﬁcation with noisy training labels when graph structure is available
during training but inference is limited to node attributes. GB-LCSS forms disjoint, label-agnostic regions in a graph
contrastive representation and converts leave-one-out local consensus into node-speciﬁc targets for the deployed MLP.
Across ﬁve datasets and ﬁve noise settings, it achieves the highest or tied-highest ﬁve-seed mean in 24 of 25 settings
among seven noise-robust baselines using the same deployed MLP architecture. Averaged over these settings, GB-LCSS
also has higher mean accuracy than PosteL, ALS, and SALS under the common protocol.
Granular balls organize supervision into a bounded set of disjoint regions. Within each ball, a single label histogram
is reused by subtracting each training node’s own vote. Alternative neighborhood and clustering rules also support
local consensus. As a secondary extension, a graph teacher trained on the same targets supplies predictions over all
nodes and improves the MLP student’s mean accuracy under the evaluated protocol without changing the deployment
interface.
The experiments use synthetic label noise, clean validation labels, and transductive evaluation on ﬁxed graphs;
target construction also depends on a pretrained and frozen graph representation. Future work should examine real
annotation noise, model selection without clean validation labels, inductive inference for new nodes, and robustness
across representation-pretraining runs.
